\section{Conclusion and Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We examine minority participation in online moral discussion through
reception, comment--reply exchanges, and reader responses. In
high-agreement AITA discussions, minority comments receive lower scores yet
attract more replies, and challenging replies frequently articulate moral
reasons absent from their parent comments. In the survey, readers in
\emph{Balanced} report greater consideration of alternative judgments and
lower mean judgment certainty than readers in \emph{Highly Scored}, while
observed majority-alignment proportions are similar. These findings
suggest that approval alone does not capture the role of dissent in
discussions of competing moral judgments.

Our contribution is to distinguish the social acceptance of a judgment
from the reasons articulated in response to it and the alternatives
readers report considering. Separating reply stance from added reasons
shows how a judgment can be rejected while the exchange around it makes
further grounds for judgment explicit. This finding connects research on
minority dissent and divergent thinking or group cognitive complexity
\cite{nemeth1986differential,curseu2012socially} to reasoning articulated
within online moral exchanges. A minority comment's contribution may lie
partly in the responses that contest it, making the comment--reply
exchange a useful unit of analysis alongside the isolated comment.
Additional reason categories describe the content of these exchanges, but do not necessarily indicate their quality.

The distinction between blaming and defending dissent further shows why
minority status alone is insufficient to characterize these exchanges.
Under high agreement, blaming dissent attracts more extensive reply
engagement, whereas challenges to defending dissent have the highest
observed rate of added reasons. Accounts of public blame as subject to
demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially} offer a possible
interpretation of the engagement asymmetry. The different directional
patterns distinguish how much interaction dissent receives from what
reasons are articulated within it.

The survey findings add a distinction between considering an alternative
and endorsing it. Consistent with research on disagreement and the
articulation of opposing reasons \cite{price2002disagreement}, the survey
motivates evaluating readers' consideration alongside the judgment they
select. It complements the reply analysis but does not establish that
the added reasons observed in replies explain the survey differences:
exposure to those specific reasons was not isolated. Tracing which
reasons readers encounter and use would help connect these two levels of
analysis.

For platforms, these findings motivate attention to both which judgments
become visible and which reasons accompany them. Making majority and
minority threads accessible could be complemented by highlighting the
reasons shared, disputed, or added in their exchanges.
\citet{kriplean2012considerit} provide a design precedent through shared
pro and con reasons and reflection on tradeoffs. Future experiments could
compare opposing threads with and without an accompanying organization of
their reasons and counterarguments. Drawing on work on argument repertoire
\cite{cappella2002argument,price2002disagreement}, evaluation could examine
the reasons readers articulate for their own and opposing judgments,
alongside judgment direction and certainty. Delayed assessments could
examine retention, while follow-up discussions could assess whether greater
visibility of dissent increases hostile interaction or participation burdens.

\runinheading{Limitations.}
We report several limitations of our study. Blaming and defending dissent
occur in different posts, so their contrasts may also reflect differences
in the situations discussed; comparisons across posts with similar topics
and conflict characteristics could assess the robustness of these patterns.
Although reception patterns recur in AITAH, both communities concern
interpersonal conflicts, leaving generalization to other settings open.
The survey covers a limited set of scenarios, varies comment content,
replies, and scores together, and uses exploratory comparisons with
unadjusted $p$-values. Larger studies could vary these components separately
and complement reported consideration with the reasons readers articulate
and retain.
