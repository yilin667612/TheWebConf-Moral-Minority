\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online communities bring together different opinions, but broad agreement can leave some participants in a small minority. How communities respond to these voices matters for whether disagreement receives consideration or primarily encounters opposition. Attention alone cannot distinguish these possibilities: a minority contribution may attract extensive discussion without receiving support. Understanding minority reception therefore requires examining both how contributions are evaluated and what kinds of responses they receive.

Prior work shows that negatively rated Reddit comments can attract more replies and larger reply trees \cite{davis2021emotional}, while voting reflects local norms and practices \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Yet a negatively rated comment is not necessarily a minority opinion. We examine reception using the distribution of expressed judgments to identify majority and minority positions, independently of the scores and replies they receive.

We study r/AmItheAsshole (AITA), where an original poster (OP) describes an everyday interpersonal conflict and commenters judge the OP's conduct using verdicts such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole''). These explicit judgments make it possible to distinguish numerical alignment from evaluation and engagement. Related work examines comment valuation as consensus develops in AITA \cite{mellody2025consensus}.

Yet minority status alone does not capture the surrounding opinion distribution: disagreeing with a nearly unanimous crowd may differ from taking the less common side in an evenly divided discussion. We therefore compare majority--minority reception across high- and low-agreement discussions, using each post's final observed verdict distribution to define these contexts.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1: Minority reception.}] How do majority--minority differences in evaluation and conversational reception vary between high- and low-agreement discussions?
\end{description}

Agreement strength, however, does not specify what a minority judgment challenges. We distinguish \emph{blaming dissent}, which assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends, from \emph{defending dissent}, which challenges the majority's blame. Both oppose the crowd, but one calls for condemnation while the other resists it. Theories of socially regulated blame suggest that criticism can itself invite scrutiny and demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}. This raises the question of whether the two directions differ in how firmly judgments are expressed and how they are received.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ2: Direction of dissent.}] Within high-agreement discussions, how do blaming and defending dissent differ in their reception and expressed certainty?
\end{description}

Differences in scores, reply volume, and expressed certainty characterize dissent and its reception, but do not reveal what the ensuing exchanges add to the discussion. A reply can challenge a judgment while introducing a new consideration, or support it without adding a different perspective. We therefore distinguish response orientation from perspective novelty and annotate top-level comment--direct reply pairs to identify and categorize perspectives absent from the parent comment.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ3: Perspectives in replies.}] How do the prevalence and types of new perspectives introduced in direct replies vary with the parent comment's majority--minority alignment and blaming--defending stance?
\end{description}

Identifying new perspectives in replies does not establish whether readers consider them when forming their own judgments. Research on minority influence suggests that dissent can broaden the approaches people consider without necessarily securing agreement \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. We therefore use a separate between-subjects survey experiment to examine whether including a minority thread changes readers' judgments and the perspectives they consider, compared with presenting only majority threads.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ4: Reader responses in a survey experiment.}] How does exposure to one majority and one minority comment thread, compared with two majority comment threads, affect readers' judgments and the perspectives they consider?
\end{description}

AITA serves as our primary setting; a supplementary comparison with r/AITAH (AITAH) assesses whether key observational patterns recur beyond it. Cross-posted stories further permit comparison around shared narratives, without equalizing audiences or exposure.

The observational analyses show that majority--minority gaps in evaluation and reply engagement generally widen with stronger agreement in AITA; annotated replies to minority judgments also contain a higher proportion of challenges. Within high-agreement AITA posts, blaming dissent receives lower mean scores, more replies, and deeper reply trees than defending dissent, alongside a higher post-averaged proportion of maximally certain judgments in the annotated sample. The directional ordering in scores and reply engagement also recurs in AITAH, although its magnitude differs. These findings establish differences in reception and expression. Separately, perspective annotations characterize new considerations introduced in observed replies, while the survey experiment examines how exposure to minority threads shapes readers' judgments and consideration of the issue.
