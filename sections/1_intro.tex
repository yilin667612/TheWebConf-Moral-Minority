\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online communities bring together different opinions, but broad agreement can leave some participants in a small minority. How communities respond to these voices matters for whether disagreement receives consideration or primarily encounters opposition. Attention alone cannot distinguish these possibilities: a minority contribution may attract extensive discussion without receiving support. Understanding minority reception therefore requires examining both how contributions are evaluated and what kinds of responses they receive.

Prior work shows that negatively rated Reddit comments can attract more replies and larger reply trees \cite{davis2021emotional}, while voting reflects local norms and practices \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Yet a negatively rated comment is not necessarily a minority opinion. We examine reception using the distribution of expressed judgments to identify majority and minority positions, independently of the scores and replies they receive.

We study r/AmItheAsshole (AITA), where an original poster (OP) describes an everyday interpersonal conflict and commenters judge the OP's conduct using verdicts such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole''). These explicit judgments make it possible to distinguish numerical alignment from evaluation and engagement. Related work examines comment valuation as consensus develops in AITA \cite{mellody2025consensus}. We combine scores and reply structure with direct-response annotations to distinguish support from challenge, excluding OP participation from the annotated response comparison.

Using each post's final observed verdict distribution, we compare high-agreement discussions, where at least $80\%$ of retained judgments favor one side, with low-agreement discussions, where neither side exceeds $60\%$, excluding ties. These groups summarize the agreement gradient (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aita-agreement-gradient}) while keeping subsequent comparisons across dissent directions and communities interpretable.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1: Minority reception.}] How do majority--minority differences in evaluation and conversational reception vary between high- and low-agreement discussions?
\end{description}

Minority judgments can also challenge different moral positions. We distinguish \emph{blaming dissent}, which assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends, from \emph{defending dissent}, which challenges the majority's blame. Both oppose the crowd, but one calls for condemnation while the other resists it. Theories of socially regulated blame suggest that public criticism itself invites scrutiny and demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}. This motivates examining whether the two directions differ in reception and in how firmly judgments are expressed, rather than treating minority voices as interchangeable. Certainty annotations characterize expressed language, not private confidence or within-person attitude change.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ2: Direction of dissent.}] Within high-agreement discussions, how do blaming and defending dissent differ in their reception and expressed certainty?
\end{description}

Reception alone does not establish what dissent contributes to a discussion. Research on minority influence links exposure to dissent with consideration of alternative approaches \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. We annotate top-level comment--direct reply pairs from the archive to identify whether a reply introduces a perspective absent from its parent comment and to categorize that perspective. Perspective annotations are independent of support/challenge labels, allowing us to distinguish the introduction of a new consideration from the direction of the response.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ3: Perspectives in replies.}] How do the prevalence and types of new perspectives introduced in direct replies vary with the parent comment's majority--minority alignment and blaming--defending stance?
\end{description}

Separately from the archival annotation analysis, we examine readers' responses in a between-subjects survey experiment. Participants view a post with either two majority comment threads or one majority and one minority comment thread, retaining a common majority thread across conditions. We measure their judgments, agreement with the comments, perceived influence, and consideration of previously unconsidered perspectives. Whereas the annotation analysis characterizes the content of observed exchanges, the survey examines readers' responses to the threads they view.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ4: Reader responses in a survey experiment.}] How does exposure to one majority and one minority comment thread, compared with two majority comment threads, affect readers' judgments and the perspectives they consider?
\end{description}

AITA serves as our primary setting. We additionally examine whether key observational patterns recur in r/AITAH (AITAH), using cross-posted stories as a supplementary comparison of reception around shared narratives. These analyses assess the scope and limits of generalizability rather than constituting a separate research question; shared narratives do not equalize audiences or exposure.

The observational analyses show that majority--minority gaps in evaluation and reply engagement generally widen with stronger agreement in AITA; annotated replies to minority judgments also contain a higher proportion of challenges. Within high-agreement AITA posts, blaming dissent receives lower mean scores, more replies, and deeper reply trees than defending dissent, alongside a higher post-averaged proportion of maximally certain judgments in the annotated sample. The directional ordering in scores and reply engagement also recurs in AITAH, although its magnitude differs. These findings establish differences in reception and expression. Separately, perspective annotations characterize new considerations introduced in observed replies, while the survey experiment examines how exposure to minority threads shapes readers' judgments and consideration of the issue.
