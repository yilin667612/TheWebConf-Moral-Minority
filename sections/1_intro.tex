\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online communities bring together people with different opinions, yet some views attract much broader agreement than others. How do communities respond to participants who express a minority view, particularly when most others agree? This question matters for understanding the space available for disagreement within a community: attracting attention is not the same as receiving support, and an extended exchange may either engage with an alternative perspective or reinforce opposition to it. Understanding the reception of minority voices therefore requires examining both how their contributions are evaluated and what kinds of responses they receive.

Prior research establishes that negative evaluation and conversational attention can coexist. Graham and Rodriguez show that Reddit voting is embedded in users' norms and social practices, rather than simply implementing the platform's prescribed criteria for evaluating content \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Davis and Graham find that downvoted comments attract more direct replies and larger reply trees than upvoted comments \cite{davis2021emotional}. These findings motivate distinguishing evaluation from attention, but a negatively rated comment need not express a numerically minority opinion. Our focus is how opinions that depart from a discussion's prevailing position are received, and how that reception varies with the level of agreement.

To examine this question, we study r/AmItheAsshole (AITA), a community in which participants judge everyday interpersonal conflicts. An original poster (OP) describes a situation, and commenters use explicit verdicts such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole'') to judge the OP's conduct. This setting lets us distinguish a comment's alignment with other judgments from the approval and attention it receives: verdicts identify the distribution of positions, while scores and replies capture different aspects of reception. Related research examines how comment scores vary with developing consensus in AITA \cite{mellody2025consensus}.

We define alignment using each post's final observed judgment distribution and compare high-agreement posts, where at least 80\% of retained judgments favor one side, with low-agreement posts, where neither side exceeds 60\%, excluding ties. We combine scores and reply structure with annotations of direct parent--reply pairs to distinguish support, challenge, and information-seeking. We also separate responses by the OP from those by other commenters. Research on moral rebels shows that reactions to principled dissent can differ between people implicated in the situation and uninvolved observers \cite{monin2008rejection}, motivating attention to who responds. In our setting, this distinction lets us examine whether challenges to minority judgments extend beyond the person under judgment. Our first question is:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1: Minority reception.}] How are minority judgments evaluated and responded to in high-agreement moral discussions, and how does their reception differ from that in low-agreement discussions?
\end{description}

Minority judgments, however, can challenge different moral positions. When most participants defend the OP, dissent assigns fault to someone the crowd largely excuses. When most participants blame the OP, dissent rejects the crowd's condemnation. We call these positions \emph{blaming dissent} and \emph{defending dissent}, respectively. Both oppose a prevailing judgment, but they differ in whether they call for blame or resist it. Treating them as a single minority category can conceal differences in the evaluations and responses they attract. Having established the reception of minority judgments across agreement levels, we therefore ask:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ2: Direction of dissent.}] Within high-agreement posts, how do blaming and defending dissent differ in their expressed certainty and in the evaluations and conversational responses they receive?
\end{description}

As part of this directional comparison, language annotations characterize expressed certainty in minority comments, allowing us to compare how firmly blaming and defending judgments are stated. This analysis concerns linguistic expression over the full observed period, rather than changes in individual attitudes or certainty over time.

We then extend the analysis to r/AITAH (AITAH), another community organized around accounts of interpersonal conflict and explicit moral judgments. This comparison examines how far the reception patterns observed in AITA extend to another community context:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ3: Community context.}] Which patterns in minority reception, including differences between blaming and defending dissent, recur in AITAH, and which differ?
\end{description}

We complemented the aggregate community comparison with qualitative case studies of stories posted in both AITA and AITAH. Examining judgments and ensuing exchanges around the same interpersonal conflict brings the context of individual discussions into view. These cases allow a closer examination of how participants articulate and respond to minority positions in particular conversations; they do not establish a uniform pattern of differences between the communities.

Our analyses show that majority--minority differences in evaluation and reply engagement are more pronounced in high-agreement than low-agreement AITA posts. Direct-response annotations excluding OP participation further indicate that minority judgments receive a higher proportion of challenging replies than majority judgments across the analyzed strata. Within high-agreement posts, blaming dissent receives lower mean scores and greater reply engagement than defending dissent. Certainty annotations also suggest a higher mean within-post proportion of comments at the highest expressed-certainty level for blaming than defending dissent in high-agreement AITA posts. AITAH exhibits the same directional ordering in scores and reply engagement, with different magnitudes.

The study contributes an account of minority reception that connects numerical alignment and agreement strength to evaluation, conversational engagement, and the content of direct responses. It further distinguishes two directions of moral dissent, showing how an aggregate minority category can obscure differences between challenging collective defense and challenging collective blame. The certainty analysis characterizes how these minority positions are expressed, while the cross-posted case studies situate judgments and responses in the context of individual exchanges. Together, the aggregate comparisons and qualitative cases provide complementary views of how online moral discussions respond to disagreement.
