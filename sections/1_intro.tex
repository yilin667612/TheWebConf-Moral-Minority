\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online communities judge everyday interpersonal conflicts, often reaching broad agreement about who is at fault. Yet agreement alone tells us little about the range of reasons a discussion makes available to readers. Minority comments may offer reasons relevant to readers' judgments without winning approval. Replies to these comments can introduce further moral considerations. Understanding minority voices therefore matters for assessing how online discussions accommodate competing moral considerations. This requires examining both how dissent is received and what develops in the exchanges around it.

Prior research shows that negatively rated Reddit comments can attract more replies and larger reply trees \cite{davis2021emotional}. Voting also reflects local norms and practices \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. These findings motivate examining evaluation and reply structure alongside the content of responses. More discussion does not necessarily indicate support. A challenging reply may introduce a consideration absent from the judgment it disputes.

r/AmItheAsshole (AITA) provides a setting for examining these distinctions. An original poster (OP) describes an interpersonal conflict. Commenters judge the OP's conduct using judgments such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole''). Research in AITA examines how the valuation of agreement and dissent varies as consensus develops \cite{mellody2025consensus}. Other work examines authors as both participants and narrators \cite{giorgi2023author}. Personal retellings are also studied as requests for feedback about one's actions \cite{haworth2021reasonability}. OPs describe conflicts from their own perspective and may seek confirmation that their conduct was justified. These accounts suggest a possible link between self-presentation and agreement.

High-agreement discussions, in which one judgment predominates, are prevalent among the AITA posts we analyze. Minority comments in these discussions challenge a widely shared judgment. Low-agreement discussions provide a comparison: taking the less common side in a divided discussion differs from opposing a nearly unanimous crowd. We compare comment scores, reply counts, and reply depth across these contexts. These analyses address \textbf{RQ1: Minority reception.} How do majority--minority differences in evaluation and conversational reception vary between high- and low-agreement discussions?

Within high-agreement discussions, minority judgments can challenge different moral positions. \emph{Blaming dissent} assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends. \emph{Defending dissent} challenges the majority's blame. Both oppose the prevailing judgment, but they respectively call for condemnation and resist it. Accounts of socially regulated blame suggest that public criticism can itself invite scrutiny and demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}. We compare the reception and expressed certainty of these two directions. Certainty annotations capture expressed language rather than private confidence. Reply annotations distinguish support for a comment's position from challenges to it, with majority comments as a reference. We ask \textbf{RQ2: Direction of dissent.} Within high-agreement discussions, how do blaming and defending dissent differ in their reception and expressed certainty?

In AITA, high-agreement minority comments receive lower scores but more replies and deeper reply threads than majority comments, with larger gaps than under low agreement. The reply-count gap also persists among comments posted later in the first 24 hours. This greater engagement motivates examining the moral considerations introduced in these exchanges. We annotate comment--reply pairs for \emph{additional moral considerations}: reasons introduced by a reply that are absent from its parent comment. Addition is measured independently of the reply orientation examined in RQ2. Comparing these exchanges across parent alignment and moral stance addresses \textbf{RQ3: Moral considerations in replies.} How do the prevalence and types of additional moral considerations in replies vary with reply orientation and the parent comment's alignment and stance?

We then examine readers' judgment certainty alongside the moral considerations they report. Research on minority influence suggests that dissent can encourage consideration of alternative approaches without necessarily securing agreement \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. A between-subjects survey experiment compares readers shown two majority comment threads with readers shown one majority and one minority thread. Readers see the post, threads, and scores, but no majority/minority labels or agreement percentages. We measure readers' self-reported certainty in their own judgment, distinct from the expressed certainty annotated in comments. Readers also report new moral considerations and the perceived influence of each comment. The comparison concerns the combined presentation of thread content and social information. We ask \textbf{RQ4: Reader responses.} How do readers' judgment certainty, reported moral considerations, and perceived comment influence differ between the two comment-display conditions?

We also examine r/AITAH (AITAH), where similar patterns emerge in majority--minority reception gaps and differences in reception between blaming and defending dissent.

The study connects the reception of minority voices, the moral considerations introduced in replies, and readers' responses to dissent. Distinguishing blaming from defending dissent makes the moral direction of opposition explicit. In the annotated high-agreement sample, replies to minority comments more often introduce additional moral considerations than replies to majority comments. In the survey, readers shown one majority and one minority thread report a broader range of moral considerations than those shown two majority threads. Together, these findings help evaluate dissent beyond whether it wins approval and inform how comment displays might make competing reasons accessible.
