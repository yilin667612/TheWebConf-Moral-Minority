\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online communities regularly judge everyday interpersonal conflicts: whether someone acted unfairly, owes an apology, or deserves support. Even when these discussions converge on a clear verdict, some participants disagree. The reception of these minority judgments matters because it reveals how a community responds to challenges to its prevailing view. A minority comment may receive little approval yet attract an extended exchange; that exchange may offer support, seek clarification, or reinforce opposition to the judgment. Understanding the place of minority voices therefore requires examining both how they are evaluated and what kinds of responses they receive.

Prior research establishes that negative evaluation and conversational attention can coexist. Graham and Rodriguez show that Reddit voting is embedded in users' norms and social practices, rather than simply implementing the platform's prescribed criteria for evaluating content \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Davis and Graham find that downvoted comments attract more direct replies and larger reply trees than upvoted comments \cite{davis2021emotional}. This establishes a tension between evaluation and attention, but identifies divergence through ratings rather than a comment's position within an independently measured distribution of judgments. More directly, Mellody examines how the scores of agreeing and dissenting judgments vary with developing consensus in r/AmItheAsshole (AITA), finding a nonlinear relationship \cite{mellody2025consensus}. Consensus-dependent evaluation is thus an existing research question on which we build.

We extend this work by examining the conversational reception accompanying those evaluations. Reply volume alone cannot establish whether a minority judgment receives support or challenge, and the distinction matters especially when most participants agree. We compare majority and minority judgments across high- and low-agreement discussions, combining scores and reply structure with annotations of direct parent--reply pairs. We also distinguish responses from the person whose conduct is being judged from responses by other commenters. Research on moral rebels shows that reactions to principled dissent can differ between people implicated in the situation and uninvolved observers \cite{monin2008rejection}, motivating attention to who responds. In our setting, this distinction lets us examine whether challenges to minority judgments extend beyond the person under judgment.

AITA provides a setting in which to examine these questions. An original poster (OP) describes an interpersonal situation, and commenters use explicit verdicts such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole'') to judge the OP's conduct. Grouping verdicts by whether they assign fault to the OP allows us to identify minority positions separately from their scores. We define alignment using each post's final observed judgment distribution and compare high-agreement posts, where at least 80\% of retained judgments favor one side, with low-agreement posts, where neither side exceeds 60\%, excluding ties. Unlike Mellody's analysis of evolving consensus among comments with scores of at least one, we retain zero and negative scores and characterize reception within these final-distribution groups. Our first question is:

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1: Minority reception.}] How are minority judgments evaluated and responded to in high-agreement moral discussions, and how does their reception differ from that in low-agreement discussions?
\end{description}

Minority judgments, however, can challenge different moral positions. When most participants defend the OP, dissent assigns fault to someone the crowd largely excuses. When most participants blame the OP, dissent rejects the crowd's condemnation. We call these positions \emph{blaming dissent} and \emph{defending dissent}, respectively. Both oppose a prevailing judgment, but they differ in whether they call for blame or resist it. Treating them as a single minority category can conceal differences in the evaluations and responses they attract. Having established the reception of minority judgments across agreement levels, we therefore ask:

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{RQ2: Direction of dissent.}] Within high-agreement posts, how do blaming and defending dissent differ in the evaluations and conversational responses they receive?
\end{description}

Alongside this reception analysis, we examine how confidently the two directions of dissent are expressed. Supplementary language annotations characterize expressed certainty in minority comments, allowing us to compare how firmly blaming and defending judgments are stated. This analysis concerns linguistic expression over the full observed period, rather than changes in individual attitudes or certainty over time.

We then extend the analysis to r/AITAH (AITAH), another community organized around accounts of interpersonal conflict and explicit moral judgments. This comparison examines how far the reception patterns observed in AITA extend to another community context:

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{RQ3: Community context.}] Which patterns in minority reception, including differences between blaming and defending dissent, recur in AITAH, and which differ?
\end{description}

We complemented the aggregate community comparison with qualitative case studies of stories posted in both AITA and AITAH. Examining judgments and ensuing exchanges around the same interpersonal conflict brings the context of individual discussions into view. These cases allow a closer examination of how participants articulate and respond to minority positions in particular conversations; they do not establish a uniform pattern of differences between the communities.

Our analyses show that majority--minority differences in evaluation and reply engagement are more pronounced in high-agreement than low-agreement AITA posts. Direct-response annotations excluding OP participation further indicate that minority judgments receive a higher proportion of challenging replies than majority judgments across the analyzed strata. Within high-agreement posts, blaming dissent receives lower mean scores and greater reply engagement than defending dissent. Supplementary annotations also suggest a higher mean within-post proportion of comments at the highest expressed-certainty level for blaming than defending dissent in high-agreement AITA posts. AITAH exhibits the same directional ordering in scores and reply engagement, with different magnitudes.

The study contributes an account of minority reception that connects numerical alignment and agreement strength to evaluation, conversational engagement, and the content of direct responses. It further distinguishes two directions of moral dissent, showing how an aggregate minority category can obscure differences between challenging collective defense and challenging collective blame. The supplementary certainty analysis characterizes how these minority positions are expressed, while the cross-posted case studies situate judgments and responses in the context of individual exchanges. Together, the aggregate comparisons and qualitative cases provide complementary views of how online moral discussions respond to disagreement.
