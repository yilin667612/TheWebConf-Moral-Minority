Building on prior work on agreement and dissent, we examine variation in minority reception along two dimensions: the discussion's level of agreement and the moral direction of dissent. High-agreement discussions, in which one judgment predominates, are prevalent among the AITA posts we analyze. Low-agreement discussions provide a comparison: taking the less common side in a divided discussion differs from opposing a nearly unanimous crowd. Within high-agreement discussions, we further distinguish \emph{blaming dissent}, which assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends, from \emph{defending dissent}, which challenges the majority's blame. Both oppose the prevailing judgment, but they respectively call for condemnation and resist it. Accounts of socially regulated blame suggest that public criticism can itself invite scrutiny and demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}. We ask 
\textbf{RQ1: Reception of minority comments.} How are minority comments received, and how does their reception vary between high- and low-agreement discussions and, within high-agreement discussions, between blaming and defending dissent? Using majority comments as a reference, we examine the reception of minority comments through comment scores, reply counts, and reply depth.

To characterize these directional patterns, we also annotate expressed certainty, with majority comments as a reference. Certainty annotations capture expressed language rather than private confidence. Together, these analyses examine when reception gaps are more pronounced and how they differ with the moral position that dissent challenges.

The greater engagement around minority judgments raises two connected questions: how replies respond to these judgments, and what reasons those exchanges introduce. We first classify reply stance, distinguishing support for the parent comment's position from challenges to it, while retaining information-seeking and other replies as separate categories. We then annotate \emph{additional moral reasons}: reasons expressed in a reply that are absent from its parent comment. We measure addition separately from stance, allowing both supportive and challenging replies to introduce further considerations. We ask
{\bf RQ2: Minority discourse.} How are minority judgments expressed and discussed?
We examine three aspects: the certainty expressed in minority comments; whether replies support or challenge their judgments; and the moral reasons covered by comments and their replies, including whether replies introduce reasons absent from the parent comment.

On Reddit and other online platforms, comment rankings use votes and engagement signals to shape which contributions readers encounter first. Such displays can foreground majority opinion, leaving minority views less visible. We therefore explore a display intervention that promotes a minority comment so readers encounter both sides of a moral disagreement.
In a between-subjects survey experiment, we compare a display containing two highly scored majority comments with a balanced display in which the highest-scoring minority comment replaces the second majority comment. Both conditions retain the comments’ original wording, scores, and accompanying reply threads. We ask {\bf RQ3: Displaying both sides.} How does promoting a minority comment alongside a majority comment affect readers’ judgment certainty and consideration of alternative moral reasons?
This exploratory study examines readers’ reported consideration of multiple sides, new moral reasons, and alternative judgments, alongside their judgment certainty and the perceived influence of individual comments.

%We then examine readers' judgment certainty alongside the moral reasons they report. Research on minority influence suggests that dissent can encourage consideration of alternative approaches without necessarily securing agreement \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. A between-subjects survey experiment compares readers shown two majority comment threads with readers shown one majority and one minority thread. Readers see the post, threads, and scores, but no majority/minority labels or agreement percentages. We measure readers' self-reported certainty in their own judgment, distinct from the expressed certainty annotated in comments. Readers also report new moral reasons and the perceived influence of each comment. The comparison concerns the combined presentation of thread content and social information. We ask \textbf{RQ3: Reader responses.} How do readers' judgment certainty, reported moral reasons, and perceived comment influence differ between the two comment-display conditions?

We also examine r/AITAH (AITAH), where similar patterns emerge in majority--minority reception gaps and differences in reception between blaming and defending dissent.

The study connects variation in minority reception across agreement levels and moral directions, the moral reasons introduced in replies, and readers' responses to dissent. Distinguishing blaming from defending dissent makes the moral direction of opposition explicit. In the annotated high-agreement sample, replies to minority comments more often introduce additional moral reasons than replies to majority comments. In the survey, readers shown one majority and one minority thread report a broader range of moral reasons than those shown two majority threads. Together, these findings help evaluate dissent beyond whether it wins approval and inform how comment displays might make competing reasons accessible.
