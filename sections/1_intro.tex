\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online communities bring together different opinions, but broad agreement can leave some participants in a small minority. How communities respond to these voices matters for whether disagreement receives consideration or primarily encounters opposition. Attention alone cannot distinguish these possibilities: a minority contribution may attract extensive discussion without receiving support. Understanding minority reception therefore requires examining both how contributions are evaluated and what kinds of responses they receive.

Prior work shows that negatively rated Reddit comments can attract more replies and larger reply trees \cite{davis2021emotional}, while voting reflects local norms and practices \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Yet a negatively rated comment is not necessarily a minority opinion. We examine reception using the distribution of expressed judgments to identify majority and minority positions, independently of the scores and replies they receive.

We study r/AmItheAsshole (AITA), where an original poster (OP) describes an everyday interpersonal conflict and commenters judge the OP's conduct using verdicts such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole''). These explicit judgments make it possible to distinguish numerical alignment from evaluation and engagement. Related work examines comment valuation as consensus develops in AITA \cite{mellody2025consensus}.

We first situate minority reception within the surrounding distribution of opinions. Disagreeing with a nearly unanimous crowd and taking the less common side in an evenly divided discussion represent different contexts for dissent. Using each post's final observed verdict distribution, we compare majority--minority reception across high- and low-agreement discussions: \textbf{RQ1 (Minority reception).} How do majority--minority differences in evaluation and conversational reception vary between high- and low-agreement discussions?

Within high-agreement discussions, we bring the moral direction of dissent into focus. We distinguish \emph{blaming dissent}, which assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends, from \emph{defending dissent}, which challenges the majority's blame. These two forms of opposition respectively call for condemnation and resist it. Theories of socially regulated blame suggest that criticism can itself invite scrutiny and demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}. This distinction motivates a closer examination of how firmly judgments in each direction are expressed and how they are received: \textbf{RQ2 (Direction of dissent).} Within high-agreement discussions, how do blaming and defending dissent differ in their reception and expressed certainty?

Alongside evaluation and engagement, we examine the substantive content of the exchanges surrounding these judgments. Replies can introduce additional considerations while either supporting or challenging their parent comment. Treating response orientation and perspective novelty as independent dimensions, we annotate top-level comment--direct reply pairs to identify and categorize these new perspectives. This analysis examines how the content of replies varies across majority and minority comments and across blaming and defending stances: \textbf{RQ3 (Perspectives in replies).} How do the prevalence and types of new perspectives introduced in direct replies vary with the parent comment's majority--minority alignment and blaming--defending stance?

We also examine the role of minority threads from the reader's perspective. Research on minority influence suggests that dissent can broaden the approaches people consider without necessarily securing agreement \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. Complementing the annotation of observed exchanges, a separate between-subjects survey experiment examines how readers respond when a minority thread is included alongside a majority thread. We compare their judgments and the perspectives they consider with those of readers shown two majority threads: \textbf{RQ4 (Reader responses in a survey experiment).} How does exposure to one majority and one minority comment thread, compared with two majority comment threads, affect readers' judgments and the perspectives they consider?

AITA serves as our primary setting; a supplementary comparison with r/AITAH (AITAH) assesses whether key observational patterns recur beyond it. Cross-posted stories further permit comparison around shared narratives, without equalizing audiences or exposure.

The observational analyses show that majority--minority gaps in evaluation and reply engagement generally widen with stronger agreement in AITA; annotated replies to minority judgments also contain a higher proportion of challenges. Within high-agreement AITA posts, blaming dissent receives lower mean scores, more replies, and deeper reply trees than defending dissent, alongside a higher post-averaged proportion of maximally certain judgments in the annotated sample. The directional ordering in scores and reply engagement also recurs in AITAH, although its magnitude differs. These findings establish differences in reception and expression. Separately, perspective annotations characterize new considerations introduced in observed replies, while the survey experiment examines how exposure to minority threads shapes readers' judgments and consideration of the issue.
