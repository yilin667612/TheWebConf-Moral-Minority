\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online communities bring together different opinions, but broad agreement can leave some participants in a small minority. How communities respond to these voices matters for whether disagreement receives consideration or primarily encounters opposition. Attention alone cannot distinguish these possibilities: a minority contribution may attract extensive discussion without receiving support. Understanding minority reception therefore requires examining both how contributions are evaluated and what kinds of responses they receive.

Prior work shows that negatively rated Reddit comments can attract more replies and larger reply trees \cite{davis2021emotional}, while voting reflects local norms and practices \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Yet a negatively rated comment is not necessarily a minority opinion. We examine reception using the distribution of expressed judgments to identify majority and minority positions, independently of the scores and replies they receive.

We study r/AmItheAsshole (AITA), where an original poster (OP) describes an everyday interpersonal conflict and commenters judge the OP's conduct using verdicts such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole''). These explicit judgments make it possible to distinguish numerical alignment from evaluation and engagement. Related work examines comment valuation as consensus develops in AITA \cite{mellody2025consensus}. We combine scores and reply structure with direct-response annotations to distinguish support from challenge, excluding OP participation from the annotated response comparison.

Using each post's final observed verdict distribution, we compare high-agreement discussions, where at least $80\%$ of retained judgments favor one side, with low-agreement discussions, where neither side exceeds $60\%$, excluding ties. These groups summarize the agreement gradient while keeping subsequent comparisons across dissent directions and communities interpretable.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1: Minority reception.}] How does the evaluation and conversational reception of minority judgments differ between high- and low-agreement discussions?
\end{description}

Minority judgments can also challenge different moral positions. We distinguish \emph{blaming dissent}, which assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends, from \emph{defending dissent}, which challenges the majority's blame. Both oppose the crowd, but one calls for condemnation while the other resists it. Theories of socially regulated blame suggest that public criticism itself invites scrutiny and demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}. This motivates examining whether the two directions differ in reception and in how firmly judgments are expressed, rather than treating minority voices as interchangeable.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ2: Direction of dissent.}] Within high-agreement posts, how do blaming and defending dissent differ in expressed certainty, evaluation, and conversational responses?
\end{description}

Certainty annotations characterize expressed language, not private confidence or within-person attitude change. We then extend the analysis to r/AITAH (AITAH) to examine which patterns recur beyond AITA. Cross-posted stories additionally let us compare how minority judgments are received around the same narrative in the two communities, without assuming identical audiences or exposure.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ3: Community context.}] Which patterns in minority reception and expressed certainty recur in AITAH, and how does minority reception differ between communities for stories posted in both?
\end{description}

Our findings reveal three patterns. First, majority--minority gaps in evaluation and reply engagement generally widen with stronger agreement in AITA; annotated replies to minority judgments also contain a higher proportion of challenges. Second, within high-agreement AITA posts, blaming dissent receives lower mean scores, more replies, and deeper reply trees than defending dissent. The annotated sample also shows a higher post-averaged proportion of maximally certain judgments for blaming dissent. Third, the directional ordering in scores and reply engagement recurs in AITAH, although its magnitude differs. Together, these findings show why minority status alone is insufficient: the reception and expression of moral dissent depend not only on opposing the majority, but also on whether that opposition challenges collective defense or collective blame.
