Online crowds do not treat all minority opinions in the same way. In a divided discussion, the minority opinion is simply one side of an ongoing debate. But when most people agree, a minority opinion directly challenges the crowd. It then becomes dissent.

Core research question: How are minority opinions expressed and treated in online moral discussions? 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does the difference in how majority and minority
    opinions are treated vary between low-agreement and
    high-agreement posts?

    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How do users respond to minority opinions in
    high-agreement posts?

    \item \textbf{RQ3:} Does the treatment of minority opinions depend
    on whether they blame or defend the post author?

    \item \textbf{RQ4:} Do minority opinions develop differently in
    AITA and AITAH?
\end{enumerate}

Online platforms make minority opinions visible, but visibility does not necessarily mean equal treatment. A dissenting comment may be rejected through downvotes while simultaneously attracting substantial attention through replies. Conversely, a widely supported comment may receive little direct engagement because it leaves little to contest. Understanding how crowds treat minority voices therefore requires separating approval from conversational attention.
Not every numerically smaller opinion, however, constitutes meaningful dissent. When opinion is evenly divided, the smaller side is simply one participant in an unresolved debate. When more than 80\% of a crowd supports one judgment, expressing the opposite view becomes a challenge to an established consensus. Minority status is thus relational: the same judgment can be an ordinary competing opinion in a divided discussion and consequential dissent in a strong-consensus discussion.
We study this distinction in two Reddit communities devoted to interpersonal moral judgment, r/AmItheAsshole (AITA) and r/AITAH. The comparison also provides an institutional contrast. AITA formalizes the prevailing judgment as an official verdict flair approximately 18 hours after posting, whereas AITAH has no comparable rule. Together, these settings allow us to examine when minority judgments become consequential, how crowds respond to them, and how dissent changes as consensus develops over time.

\subsection{Research Questions}

\textbf{RQ1. High-agreement vs Low-agreement: When does a minority judgment become consequential dissent?}
Does the treatment of minority comments depend on the strength of crowd consensus? Specifically, are majority–minority differences larger in high-agreement posts than in low-agreement posts?

\textbf{RQ2. Reply/Challenge behavior: How do crowds treat dissent against strong consensus?}
Are minority judgments sanctioned through voting while being amplified through replies and deeper discussion? Does dissent primarily initiate conversation, or does it also intensify exchanges once they have begun?

\textbf{RQ3. Blaming/Defending: Does the moral direction of dissent matter?}
Are comments that blame someone whom the crowd has largely absolved treated differently from comments that defend someone whom the crowd has largely condemned?

\textbf{RQ4. AITA/AITAH: How does dissent change as consensus develops and becomes formalized?}
How do the prevalence and expressed certainty of minority judgments evolve over time? Do these dynamics differ between AITA, where a verdict is formally displayed after 18 hours, and AITAH, where no official verdict is imposed?


\subsection{Method}
We analyze more than 15 million top-level judgment comments: over 11 million from AITA and over 4 million from AITAH. Judgments are grouped into two opposing categories: YA, indicating that the focal actor is at fault, and NA, indicating that the actor is not at fault.

Posts are classified by their YA share:
High-agreement -- YA share below 20\% or above 80\% versus Low-agreement -- YA share between 40\% and 60\%.
Within each post, a comment is classified as majority or minority according to whether its judgment agrees with the dominant side. In high-agreement posts, minority comments represent dissent against strong consensus. In low-agreement posts, the minority label identifies the numerically smaller side of a divided discussion and serves as a comparison condition.

We examine four dimensions of treatment:
voting outcomes, measured by comment score;
conversational response, measured by reply probability, reply count, and reply-tree depth;
reception of the resulting discussion, measured by the aggregate score of the reply subtree;
expressed certainty and temporal change, using certainty annotations from approximately 1,000 large threads.
We also distinguish blaming dissent—YA judgments in NA-dominant posts—from defending dissent—NA judgments in YA-dominant posts.

\subsection{Findings and Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Minority status becomes consequential only against a consensus.} In high-agreement posts, minority comments receive substantially lower scores and substantially more replies than majority comments. In low-agreement posts these majority–minority differences are small or absent. The crowd therefore responds not to numerical rarity but to a judgment's position against a clear collective verdict, which is what separates ordinary disagreement from dissent.

    This claim rests on a measurement choice. Existing work on the attention–approval split infers norm deviation from a comment's own low score \cite{davis2021emotional}, which leaves deviance and sanction entangled in a single variable. We instead read a comment's substantive moral judgment against the dominant judgment of its post, so that dissent is defined by content and its treatment by score, replies, and depth. Together with the low-agreement cohort as a control—where the two sides are near-symmetric by construction—this decoupling is what makes the comparison above possible; under a score-based definition of deviance it is unavailable even in principle.

    \item \textbf{The crowd votes against the dissenter but not against the discussion.} Minority comments receive much lower scores, including negative average scores in AITA, yet are three to five times more likely to receive replies and to seed reply subtrees approximately six times deeper on average. Attention and approval thus diverge on the same comments: the judgments the crowd votes down are the ones it answers. The divergence also appears within voting itself. A minority comment's own score and the aggregate score of the reply subtree it seeds move apart: even when the comment is scored negatively, the subtree rooted in it can accumulate a positive total. Our data do not identify what that positive total registers—it may reflect the reception of the discussion, or approval of the replies that rebut the dissent—but under either reading the negative evaluation stops at the initiating judgment and does not extend to the discussion it produces.

    \item \textbf{Dissent is directionally asymmetric.} Blaming an OP the crowd absolved is punished more heavily, yet draws roughly 1.7–1.9 times more discussion, than defending an OP the crowd condemned. Blaming judgments against a lenient consensus provoke confrontation; defending judgments of a condemned OP are more likely to be ignored. Sanction and attention are thus not two readings of a single severity—the moral direction of the challenge moves them apart, connecting the treatment of dissent to the asymmetry between blame and exculpation in moral judgment.

    \item \textbf{Formalizing a consensus does not close down dissent.} AITA displays an official verdict approximately 18 hours after posting and AITAH does not, and a subset of stories appears in both communities, giving the same narrative two independent crowds. Neither comparison shows the institution suppressing the minority: no discontinuity in expressed certainty appears at the verdict threshold, and the community with a formal verdict sustains the larger minority share. Minority certainty does rise gradually as discussions age in AITA, though not in AITAH, and greater certainty earns no additional voting reward—a pattern more consistent with gradual change in the conversational environment and in who is still speaking than with an immediate verdict effect. We report these as bounds rather than as an estimated institutional effect, since the paired subsample is small and rarely reaches the verdict counts our main analyses require.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, the findings show that a minority opinion becomes consequential dissent when there is a strong crowd consensus to oppose. Once it does, the crowd rejects the judgment but engages the challenge—extracting conversation from dissent while leaving much of its social cost with the speaker.
