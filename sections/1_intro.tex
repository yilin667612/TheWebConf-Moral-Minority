\lx{
Research on opinion expression shows that opinion climate and
anticipated sanctions are associated with willingness to express
minority views~\cite{matthes2010spiral,neubaum2018sanctions}.
On Reddit, negatively rated comments can attract more replies
and larger reply trees than positively rated
comments~\cite{davis2021emotional}, indicating that discussion
does not necessarily imply approval. The strength of consensus
also matters: dissenting comments are rated more favorably than
agreeing comments when consensus is
moderate~\cite{mellody2025consensus}. Separately, experiments
with Facebook news posts show that displaying agreeing versus
disagreeing comment pairs changes readers' patterns of
attention~\cite{dutceac2022cueing}.

Comments receive community votes and
can generate nested reply threads. We define majority and minority
positions relative to the distribution of judgments within each
discussion: majority comments align with the dominant judgment,
while minority comments oppose it.

This requires examining both how dissent is received and what develops in the exchanges around it.

\lx{
Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} provides an overview of our study in three
parts. We first ask \textbf{RQ1: Reception of minority comments.}
How are minority comments received across agreement levels and,
within high-agreement discussions, when they blame or defend the
OP? We analyze archived AITA discussions, using majority comments
as a reference. We measure community evaluation through each
comment's net vote score (upvotes minus downvotes), and conversational
engagement through its number of direct replies and the maximum
depth of its nested reply thread. We compare discussions with a
strongly dominant judgment against more evenly divided discussions.
Within high-agreement discussions, we further distinguish
\emph{blaming dissent}, which assigns fault to an OP whom the
majority defends, from \emph{defending dissent}, which defends an
OP whom the majority blames.

We next examine the content of minority comments and the exchanges
around them. 
\jjnote{Readers might expect the examination of the content of minority comments to go first before the analysis of how they're received. Do we have a good reason why we place the content analysis as the second RQ? It might be good to mention that reason here.}
\textbf{RQ2: Minority discourse.} How are minority
judgments expressed and discussed? We annotate expressed certainty
in comment language, reply stance toward the parent comment, and
the moral reasons covered by comments and replies. We compare
majority, blaming minority, and defending minority comments, and
examine expressed certainty in successive comments as discussions
develop. The example in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} illustrates why
reply stance and moral reasons are examined separately: supportive
and challenging replies can both introduce considerations absent
from the parent comment.
}

Finally, we explore an intervention that makes a minority view
more visible to readers. 
\jjnote{The sentence above implies that minority views are currently not as visible as we wish them to be, but is that what's observed from answers to RQ1? If so, shall we mention it here to motivate/justify RQ3?}
\textbf{RQ3: Balanced comment displays.}
How does promoting a minority comment alongside a majority comment
affect \lx{readers’ consideration of alternative moral reasons and the certainty of their final judgments?}
In an exploratory survey experiment with 80 readers, we randomly
assign participants to one of four AITA posts and one of the two
comment displays shown in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}.
%In an exploratory survey experiment,
%we randomly assign readers to one of the two displays shown in
%Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}. 
The \emph{Highly Scored} condition presents
two highly scored comments, both majority-aligned in the selected
posts; the \emph{Balanced} condition replaces the second with the
highest-scoring minority comment. Each selected comment retains
its original wording, score, and accompanying reply thread. 
\lx{Readers report their consideration of multiple sides, new moral reasons, and alternative judgments, alongside the perceived influence of individual comments. We also assess judgment certainty to examine whether exposure to competing judgments is accompanied by firmer or more tentative final judgments.
}
%Readers report their judgment certainty, consideration of alternative views and moral reasons, and the perceived influence of individual comments.


Our findings reveal three key insights. \textbf{(1)} In high-agreement AITA discussions, minority comments receive lower net vote scores but approximately $4.9$ times as many direct replies as majority comments. Distinguishing blaming from defending dissent further reveals further differences: blaming dissent attracts more extensive reply engagement. \textbf{(2)} Separating reply stance from added moral reasons shows that challenging replies more often introduce additional reasons than supportive replies. \lx{\textbf{(3)} In our exploratory experiment, readers shown Balanced displays report greater consideration of why someone might reach an alternative judgment, alongside lower judgment certainty, than those shown Highly Scored displays.}
These findings show how minority voices can contribute to exchanges
of moral reasons despite receiving little approval. 
They suggest that making minority views more visible could encourage critical
thinking in online communities by prompting readers to examine
competing reasons and consider alternative judgments.
