\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online communities judge everyday interpersonal conflicts, often reaching broad agreement about who is at fault. Yet agreement alone tells us little about the range of reasons a discussion makes available to readers. Minority comments may offer reasons relevant to readers' judgments without winning approval. Replies to these comments can introduce further moral reasons. Understanding minority voices therefore matters for assessing how online discussions accommodate competing moral reasons. This requires examining both how dissent is received and what develops in the exchanges around it.

Prior research shows that negatively rated Reddit comments can attract more replies and larger reply trees \cite{davis2021emotional}. Voting also reflects local norms and practices \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. These findings motivate examining evaluation and reply structure alongside the content of responses. More discussion does not necessarily indicate support. A challenging reply may introduce a consideration absent from the judgment it disputes.

r/AmItheAsshole (AITA) provides a setting for examining these distinctions. An original poster (OP) describes an interpersonal conflict. Commenters judge the OP's conduct using judgments such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole''). Research in AITA examines how the valuation of agreement and dissent varies as consensus develops \cite{mellody2025consensus}. Other work examines authors as both participants and narrators \cite{giorgi2023author}. Personal retellings are also studied as requests for feedback about one's actions \cite{haworth2021reasonability}. OPs describe conflicts from their own perspective and may seek confirmation that their conduct was justified. These accounts suggest a possible link between self-presentation and agreement.

Building on prior work on agreement and dissent, we examine variation in minority reception along two dimensions: the discussion's level of agreement and the moral direction of dissent. High-agreement discussions, in which one judgment predominates, are prevalent among the AITA posts we analyze. Low-agreement discussions provide a comparison: taking the less common side in a divided discussion differs from opposing a nearly unanimous crowd. Within high-agreement discussions, we further distinguish \emph{blaming dissent}, which assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends, from \emph{defending dissent}, which challenges the majority's blame. Both oppose the prevailing judgment, but they respectively call for condemnation and resist it. Accounts of socially regulated blame suggest that public criticism can itself invite scrutiny and demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}. We ask

Research on minority influence suggests that dissent can encourage consideration of alternative approaches without necessarily securing agreement \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. A between-subjects survey experiment compares readers shown two majority comment threads with readers shown one majority and one minority thread. Readers see the post, threads, and scores, but no majority/minority labels or agreement percentages. We measure readers' self-reported certainty in their own judgment, distinct from the expressed certainty annotated in comments. Readers also report new moral reasons and the perceived influence of each comment. The comparison concerns the combined presentation of thread content and social information. We ask \textbf{RQ3: Reader responses.} How do readers' judgment certainty, reported moral reasons, and perceived comment influence differ between the two comment-display conditions?

We also examine r/AITAH (AITAH), where similar patterns emerge in majority--minority reception gaps and differences in reception between blaming and defending dissent.

The study connects variation in minority reception across agreement levels and moral directions, the moral reasons introduced in replies, and readers' responses to dissent. Distinguishing blaming from defending dissent makes the moral direction of opposition explicit. In the annotated high-agreement sample, replies to minority comments more often introduce additional moral reasons than replies to majority comments. In the survey, readers shown one majority and one minority thread report a broader range of moral reasons than those shown two majority threads. Together, these findings help evaluate dissent beyond whether it wins approval and inform how comment displays might make competing reasons accessible.
