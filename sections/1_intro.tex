\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}


Online communities judge everyday interpersonal conflicts, often reaching broad agreement about who is at fault. Yet the prevailing verdict reveals little about how participants respond to those who disagree or what considerations arise in the exchanges around their judgments. A minority comment may receive little approval while attracting replies that support, challenge, or elaborate on its reasoning. Understanding minority voices therefore requires attention to their evaluation, the responses they receive, and the perspectives expressed in those responses.

Prior research shows that negatively rated Reddit comments can attract more replies and larger reply trees \cite{davis2021emotional}, while voting reflects local norms and practices \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Research in r/AmItheAsshole (AITA) also examines how the valuation of agreement and dissent varies as consensus develops \cite{mellody2025consensus}. We build on these accounts by connecting evaluation and reply structure to the content of responses. More discussion does not necessarily indicate support, and a challenging reply may introduce a consideration absent from the judgment it disputes.

AITA provides a setting for examining these distinctions. An original poster (OP) describes an interpersonal conflict, and commenters judge the OP's conduct using verdicts such as YTA (``You're the Asshole'') and NTA (``Not the Asshole''). High-agreement discussions, in which one judgment predominates, are prevalent among the posts we analyze in both AITA and the related community r/AITAH (AITAH). This pattern may partly reflect the first-person storytelling setting: OPs present conflicts from their own perspective and may seek confirmation that their conduct was justified. Prior AITA research examines authors as both participants and narrators \cite{giorgi2023author} and personal retellings as requests for feedback about one's actions \cite{haworth2021reasonability}. These accounts motivate, rather than establish, a link between self-presentation and agreement. In this setting, minority comments offer a way to examine how alternative judgments are expressed and received when most commenters agree. Low-agreement discussions provide a comparison: taking the less common side in a divided discussion differs from opposing a nearly unanimous crowd. We examine comment scores, direct replies, and discussion depth across these contexts, and annotate replies to distinguish support from challenge. These analyses address \textbf{RQ1 (Minority reception): How do majority--minority differences in evaluation and conversational reception vary between high- and low-agreement discussions?}

Within high-agreement discussions, minority judgments can challenge different moral positions. We distinguish \emph{blaming dissent}, which assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends, from \emph{defending dissent}, which challenges the majority's blame. Both oppose the prevailing judgment, but they respectively call for condemnation and resist it. Accounts of socially regulated blame suggest that public criticism can itself invite scrutiny and demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}, motivating a comparison of these two directions. We examine both how they are received and how firmly their judgments are expressed. Certainty annotations capture expressed language rather than private confidence. We ask \textbf{RQ2 (Direction of dissent): Within high-agreement discussions, how do blaming and defending dissent differ in their reception and expressed certainty?}

Our reception analyses show that minority comments in high-agreement discussions attract more replies and deeper reply trees than majority comments. This greater engagement motivates examining whether the exchanges around minority judgments introduce additional moral considerations. To examine their substantive content, we annotate pairs of top-level comments and their direct replies for \emph{perspective novelty}: whether a reply introduces a moral consideration absent from its parent comment, and what type of consideration it introduces. Novelty is relative to the parent, rather than to the entire discussion. We measure it separately from support/challenge, allowing supportive and challenging replies alike to introduce additional perspectives. Comparing these exchanges across parent alignment and moral stance addresses \textbf{RQ3 (Perspectives in replies): How do the prevalence and types of additional moral perspectives in direct replies vary with response orientation and the parent comment's alignment and stance?}

We then examine minority threads from the reader's perspective. Research on minority influence suggests that dissent can encourage consideration of alternative approaches without necessarily securing agreement \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. A between-subjects survey experiment compares readers shown two majority comment threads with readers shown one majority and one minority thread. Readers see the post, comment threads, and displayed scores, without majority/minority labels or agreement percentages, then report whether the threads introduced perspectives they had not considered and how much those perspectives contributed to their final decision. The comparison concerns the combined presentation of thread content and social information. We ask \textbf{RQ4 (Reader responses): Do readers encounter new perspectives, and how much do these perspectives inform their decisions, across the two exposure conditions?}

AITA is our primary setting; an additional comparison with AITAH examines whether key observational patterns recur in another community. The AITA analyses show larger majority--minority gaps in evaluation and reply engagement under high agreement than low agreement. Annotated high-agreement replies contain a higher proportion of challenges toward minority than majority judgments. Within high-agreement AITA posts, blaming dissent receives lower mean scores, more replies, and deeper reply trees than defending dissent. The annotated sample also suggests a higher post-averaged proportion of comments at the highest expressed-certainty level for blaming dissent, although this contrast remains sensitive to annotation version. The directional ordering in reception recurs in AITAH, with different magnitudes.

The study connects three aspects of minority voices in online moral discussions: how they are expressed and received, what additional considerations appear in the replies around them, and how readers respond to thread selections that include dissent. Distinguishing blaming from defending dissent makes the moral direction of opposition explicit; the perspective analysis and survey extend the inquiry from the treatment of minority voices to the content of their exchanges and readers' responses to them.
