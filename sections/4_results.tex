\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We first establish the setting in which minority judgments occur, then
examine their reception across agreement levels (RQ1), distinguish blaming
from defending dissent (RQ2), and assess which patterns recur in AITAH
(RQ3). AITA is the primary setting throughout. The directional comparison
includes both reception and expressed certainty. Cumulative participation
is reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cumulative-participation}; neither linguistic certainty nor participation
is treated as a measure of individual attitude change.

\subsection{Context: Agreement and the Direction of Minority Judgments}
\label{subsec:res-distribution}

High agreement is common in the AITA cohort, rather than an exceptional
condition. Of the $28{,}107$ posts with at least $200$ retained verdicts,
$21{,}978$ ($78.2\%$) fall in the high-agreement bands and $1{,}838$
($6.5\%$) in the low-agreement band after exact-tie exclusion.
Figure~\ref{fig:ya-share} shows a bimodal distribution of final YA share,
with the larger concentration near the NA end. Within the high-agreement
cohort, $80.6\%$ of posts are NA-dominant. Consequently, the more common
setting for dissent is one in which a minority assigns blame to an OP whom
most commenters excuse.

Post frequency and discussion volume are distinct. The red line in
Figure~\ref{fig:ya-share} shows mean retained top-level verdicts per post
within each YA-share bin. Although NA-dominant posts are more numerous,
the average verdict count is higher toward the YA end, particularly near
unanimous YA judgments. The two axes therefore distinguish how often a
judgment context occurs from how many verdict comments its posts attract.

This distribution does not establish a community-wide aversion to blame.
The stories are supplied by the people whose conduct is being judged.
Research on authors' dual roles as characters and narrators
\cite{giorgi2023author}, and on selective retelling and possible
validation-seeking \cite{haworth2021reasonability}, provides context for
the imbalance without identifying the motives of individual posters.

\subsection{Minority Reception Across Agreement Levels}
\label{subsec:res-baseline}

\paragraph{Lower scores coexist with greater reply engagement.}
In high-agreement AITA posts, equal-post mean scores are $39.55$ for
majority comments and $-10.25$ for minority comments
(Figure~\ref{fig:engagement-by-consensus}). The ordering reverses for
conversational engagement: the mean within-post direct-reply count is $0.73$
for minority comments, compared with $0.15$ for majority comments, and mean
maximum reply depth is $1.03$ rather than $0.17$. Thus, low evaluation
does not imply an absence of responses.

The differences are smaller in low-agreement posts. Majority and minority
scores average $35.89$ and $27.60$; direct-reply counts average $0.28$ and
$0.32$; and maximum reply depths average $0.36$ and $0.41$, respectively.
The mean score gap is therefore $49.80$ points under high agreement but
$8.29$ under low agreement. Contributing sample sizes appear in
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:analytical-samples}; posts without minority
comments do not contribute a minority reception value.
These archive-level comparisons establish the
reception baseline for the directional analysis, rather than treating the
coexistence of low scores and more replies as a new phenomenon. They are
descriptive contrasts, not estimates of an effect of agreement strength.
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aita-agreement-gradient} shows the corresponding
post-equal means across the full verdict distribution, including
intermediate-agreement posts.

\paragraph{Arrival timing qualifies the aggregate averages.}
Figure~\ref{fig:comment-engagement-over-time} groups comments by their
arrival hour during the first $24$ hours. Early high-agreement majority
comments have higher recorded scores, while early minority comments
receive more direct replies than later minority comments. The separation
between means, medians, and percentile bands also shows heterogeneity in
average reception across contributing posts. These are final
recorded outcomes grouped by arrival, not trajectories of scores or replies
measured repeatedly over a comment's lifetime. Arrival timing and exposure
therefore remain relevant when interpreting the aggregate contrasts.

\paragraph{OP participation is common and often begins early.}
\label{subsec:res-op-participation}
Among posts eligible for author matching, OPs contribute at least one
recorded comment in $81.0\%$ of AITA and $77.0\%$ of AITAH discussions.
Among those participating, $79.7\%$ and $75.1\%$, respectively, first comment
within an hour of submission (Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:aita-op-participation}
and~\ref{fig:aitah-op-participation}). OPs are thus often active participants,
not merely the subjects of judgment, motivating our separation of OP and
other-commenter responses. These rates exclude posts with unavailable
author identities and do not establish whether OP participation responds
to, or helps shape, the eventual verdict distribution.

\paragraph{Attention is not equivalent to support.}
\label{subsec:res-rq2}
The direct-response annotations indicate a higher challenge proportion for
minority than majority parent comments in each analyzed high-agreement
community-by-verdict-tail stratum. OP-authored pairs are excluded, so this
pattern concerns responses from other commenters rather than only the
person being judged. Together with the structural results, it suggests
that minority judgments attract both attention and contestation.

This comparison concerns the composition of sampled direct replies,
conditional on a reply being present and eligible for annotation. It does
not estimate the probability that a minority comment receives a challenge.
Moreover, the response samples cover high-agreement posts only and use a
broader sampling frame than the $200$-verdict reception cohort; they do not
establish a high--low agreement difference in challenge rates.

Taken together, these results address \textbf{RQ1}: minority judgments
combine lower evaluation with greater reply engagement, with larger
majority--minority gaps under high agreement. The annotated high-agreement
replies further indicate that attention to minority judgments includes
more contestation, not simply support.

\subsection{Blaming Versus Defending Dissent}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

\paragraph{Both directions are negatively evaluated, but engagement differs.}
Separating high-agreement minority comments by dissent direction reveals
an asymmetry that the undifferentiated minority category conceals
(Figure~\ref{fig:dissent-direction}). Blaming comments in NA-dominant posts
have an equal-post mean score of $-10.65$; defending comments in YA-dominant
posts average $-8.62$. The figure combines majority comments across both
dominant-verdict contexts, giving an equal-post mean of $39.55$.
The context-specific majority means are $39.16$ and $41.17$, respectively.
Both minority directions
therefore receive negative mean scores, with a lower mean for blaming dissent.
Minority estimates and their majority references can involve different
contributing post sets (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:analytical-samples}).

The clearer directional difference is in conversational engagement.
Blaming comments average $0.80$ direct replies, compared with $0.44$ for
defending comments---a $1.83$-fold difference calculated from unrounded
equal-post means. Their mean maximum reply depths are $1.12$ and $0.70$,
respectively, a $1.60$-fold difference. In contrast, the corresponding
majority baselines are similar: $0.15$ and $0.16$ direct replies, and depths
of $0.17$ in both post types. Challenging collective defense of the OP is thus associated
with more replies and deeper reply trees than challenging collective blame.

The top three rows show distributions of within-post means, not individual
comment outcomes. Boxes span the interquartile range, center lines mark
medians, and whiskers extend to the most extreme observations within
$1.5$ interquartile ranges of the box. White diamonds and their labels
show means over all contributing posts. Outlier points are omitted and
the linear display ranges are restricted to $[-80,125]$ for score,
$[0,2.5]$ for direct replies, and $[0,3.5]$ for depth; these display choices
do not remove posts from the summaries.

The bottom row reproduces rounded descriptive proportions from the first
direct-response annotation sample. Among replies to blaming minority
comments, $16.1\%$ support and $77.3\%$ challenge the parent judgment;
the corresponding defending proportions are $18.6\%$ and $73.7\%$.
Each minority direction contributes $2{,}000$ labeled reply pairs.
Combining the two equally sized majority-parent strata ($4{,}000$ pairs)
gives approximately $68.7\%$ support and $21.8\%$ challenge. These are
pair-weighted proportions, not post-equal estimates, and retain all four
response categories in the denominator. The response sample excludes OP
participation and has a broader frame than the $200$-verdict reception
cohort; the rows are not measurements on one matched sample.

These comparisons use different story cohorts, not alternative judgments
randomly assigned to the same story. They do not establish why blaming
dissent attracts more engagement, nor whether its additional replies are
supportive or challenging. The separate response sample provides a
descriptive blaming--defending comparison, but cannot identify the
composition of the additional replies in the full reception cohort.

\paragraph{Expressed certainty differs by dissent direction.}
\label{subsec:res-certainty-contrasts}
The directional comparison also examines how firmly blaming and defending
judgments are expressed. In high-agreement AITA posts, the mean within-post
proportion of minority comments assigned certainty level $4$ is $66.1\%$
for blaming and $58.1\%$ for defending: a difference of $8.0$ percentage
points ($95\%$ whole-post bootstrap CI $[3.9,12.2]$).
Under low agreement, the contrast is smaller and uncertain
($1.2$ percentage points; CI $[-1.6,4.1]$).
These estimates weight posts equally, as do the reception means, and use
the full observed annotation period.

The fourth row of Figure~\ref{fig:dissent-direction} shows first-day
mean certainty for high-agreement AITA. Its horizontal axis is relative
position in each post's first-$24$-hour verdict-comment sequence, divided
into ten approximately equal-count stages, rather than elapsed time.
Each curve uses a fixed post set with valid certainty observations for
that alignment or direction in every stage (sample counts in
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:analytical-samples}). Blaming certainty is descriptively
higher than defending certainty throughout these stages; both minority
curves end above their initial means. The groups are separate fixed
cohorts, and the pointwise bands do not test between-group differences
or establish within-person change.

Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-progress} provides a broader view of mean
certainty across relative comment position in the first $24$ hours,
combining all agreement levels in the broader annotation archive. In the
fixed AITA cohorts, both directions show positive post fixed-effects
trends: the fitted first-to-last-stage change is $0.039$ for blaming
($95\%$ post-bootstrap CI $[0.021,0.056]$) and $0.059$ for defending
($[0.038,0.080]$). Both survive Holm correction across the four
community--direction slopes. The profiles are not monotonic, and the
larger defending point estimate is not itself evidence of a difference
between directional trends. These pooled-agreement trajectories do not
replace the separate high/low full-period contrasts above.

The contrast requires qualification: restricting high-agreement AITA to
annotation version \texttt{v4} yields a positive estimate whose interval
includes zero. Version and post composition cannot be separated in that
comparison. Both summaries characterize expressed language, not private
confidence or increasing certainty within individuals; nor do the separate
intervals establish an agreement-by-direction interaction.
Full distributions and sensitivity checks appear in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty-comparison}.

Together, these findings address \textbf{RQ2} through both reception and
expression: blaming dissent attracts more replies and deeper reply trees
than defending dissent, while the annotated high-agreement AITA sample
also shows a higher proportion of firmly asserted judgments. The certainty
contrast remains subject to the annotation-version and validation
qualifications above.

\subsection{Reception and Expression Across Communities}
\label{subsec:res-community}

\paragraph{The agreement-level pattern recurs.}
AITAH also combines lower minority scores with greater engagement under
high agreement (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-engagement-full}).
Majority and minority equal-post mean scores are $16.14$ and $-1.89$,
respectively; direct-reply counts are $0.14$ and $0.48$; and maximum reply
depths are $0.15$ and $0.79$. Under low agreement, the reply-count difference
remains small ($0.233$ versus $0.237$), with depths of $0.37$ and $0.39$.
As in AITA, the high-agreement minority therefore occupies a distinctive
combination of lower evaluation and greater conversational engagement.

\paragraph{Directional ordering is shared, not its magnitude.}
Blaming dissent in AITAH receives a lower mean score than defending dissent
($-2.02$ versus $-0.25$), more direct replies ($0.50$ versus $0.28$), and
greater maximum reply depth ($0.81$ versus $0.51$)
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-dissent-direction}).
The blaming-to-defending reply ratio is $1.77$, compared with $1.83$ in
AITA. Both minority directions have negative equal-post mean scores in
both communities. These results show a shared
directional ordering alongside differences in recorded magnitudes.

Raw cross-community differences do not, by themselves, establish greater
tolerance, stronger normative pressure, or an effect of AITA's verdict
rule. The comparisons retain their analysis-specific observation frames
and may differ in audience, story composition, and exposure.

\paragraph{Certainty profiles do not transfer uniformly.}
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-progress} compares the two communities on
the same axes, now pooling agreement levels. AITAH blaming has a positive
fitted change of $0.036$ ($95\%$ post-bootstrap CI $[0.007,0.065]$;
Holm-adjusted $p=0.031$), whereas defending has an uncertain estimate of
$-0.015$ ($[-0.050,0.020]$; adjusted $p=0.413$). Thus, the combined
annotation sample does not support an increasing linear trend for every
community--direction group. The AITAH defending profile now includes
$155$ fixed-cohort posts across agreement levels; the separate full-period
high-agreement comparison still contains only $15$ defending posts
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high}). Significance in one group
and non-significance in another is not a test of a community difference
or community-by-direction interaction.

For \textbf{RQ3}, the recurring finding is the ordering of reception:
minority judgments attract lower scores and more reply engagement under
high agreement, and blaming dissent attracts more engagement than defending
dissent. The magnitudes vary between communities, while the certainty
profiles and exploratory slopes do not show the same pattern for every
community--direction group. The shared reception ordering does not
imply equivalent expression or reception environments, or identify their
causes. Supplementary cumulative-participation patterns appear in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cumulative-participation}.
