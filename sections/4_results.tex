\subsection{The shape of crowd verdicts}
\label{subsec:res-distribution}

Before comparing how the two sides are treated, it is worth establishing how
often each situation arises across the $28{,}107$ AITA and $8{,}217$ AITAH
high-volume posts. The distribution of the YA share is sharply bimodal
(Figure~\ref{fig:ya-share}): mass piles up against unanimous exculpation, a
smaller peak sits at unanimous inculpation, and the middle is nearly empty.
High-agreement posts outnumber low-agreement ones by more than an order of
magnitude---$78.2\%$ against $6.5\%$ in AITA, $81.6\%$ against $4.7\%$ in
AITAH---so the cohort the rest of this section concentrates on is the modal
discussion in these communities, and the low-agreement cohort is a small control
rather than the other half of the data. The two peaks are themselves very
unequal: the dominant verdict is NA in $75.7\%$ of AITA posts and $87.4\%$ of
AITAH posts, with median YA shares of $8\%$ and $6\%$, so these crowds acquit
far more often than they condemn, and the community without a formal verdict is
the more lenient of the two. Within a post the minority is correspondingly
small---a median of $4\%$ of verdicts in AITA and $6\%$ in AITAH, at or below
$2\%$ in a quarter of posts, and only about a third even at the $90$th
percentile (Table~\ref{tab:verdict-distribution}). The dissenter in a typical
high-agreement thread is not one side of a split room but one voice among dozens.

The cohorts differ not only in where they end up but in when they get there.
Figure~\ref{fig:consensus-stabilization} tracks the fraction of posts whose
running verdict leader matches the final winner at successive comment-arrival
checkpoints. The early lead is much more predictive in high-agreement posts
than in low-agreement posts. In
high-agreement posts the verdict leading after a single comment is the eventual
winner $86.5\%$ of the time, rising to $95.8\%$ after three, and $99.2\%$ never
experience a lead change at all. Low-agreement posts behave in the opposite way at
every point: the leader after one comment predicts the winner $52.1\%$ of the
time, indistinguishable from a coin flip, and even after $201$ comments only
$83.5\%$; just $38.0\%$ avoid a lead change entirely, and $75.8\%$ have stopped
changing hands by the halfway point, leaving roughly a quarter still reversing
in the second half of the discussion.

This bears directly on how the majority and minority labels should be read. We
assign them from a post's final verdict, which no commenter could have
observed, and that would be a serious objection if the final verdict were the
product of a long contest. In the high-agreement cohort it is not: the winning side
leads from the outset and never loses the lead, so the label assigned after the
fact coincides with what was on the page while people were writing. The point
is not that early commenters are unusually informative---a lopsided post makes
early agreement likely by construction---but that a dissenter in a high-agreement
thread was contradicting something already plainly visible. In low-agreement posts
no such correspondence exists, a further reason to treat that cohort as a
control rather than as a second case of dissent.

One asymmetry bears on \S\ref{subsec:res-direction} and is easy to misread.
Because NA-dominance is so much more common than YA-dominance, blaming dissent
is available in roughly three quarters of AITA posts and seven eighths of AITAH
posts, while defending dissent is confined to the remaining $24.1\%$ and
$12.4\%$. The imbalance is in posts, not in comments. Dissent is proportionally
far more common in YA-dominant threads than in NA-dominant ones, so the
defending condition draws a comparable volume of comments from many fewer
threads \textbf{[TODO: report comment counts per condition]}. What differs
between the two conditions is therefore the kind of thread each is embedded
in---defending dissent occurs where dissent is ordinary, blaming dissent where
it is rare---which is precisely why each is read against the majority comments
of its own post type rather than against each other's.

\subsection{RQ3: The direction of dissent --- blaming versus defending}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

Figure~\ref{fig:dissent-direction} compares blaming and defending dissent
(\S\ref{subsec:method}) in $1{,}500$ sampled high-agreement high-volume posts from
each community. Each condition is shown against the majority comments of the
same post type, which are not themselves classified by direction.

condition]}

\paragraph{Voting disfavors the minority in both directions.}
Across both communities, minority comments received markedly lower scores than
the majority comments of the same post type. In AITA, blaming minority
comments had a mean score of $-3.2$ against $37.2$ for majority comments in
NA-dominant posts, and defending minority comments $-2.4$ against $35.4$ in
YA-dominant posts. AITAH shows the same ordering on a compressed scale:
blaming minority comments averaged $-0.6$ against $14.7$, and defending
minority comments $1.1$ against $11.2$. The one condition in which dissent
attracts net approval---defending dissent in AITAH---still falls an order of
magnitude below the majority it is measured against, so the gap is a matter of
degree rather than of sign.

\paragraph{Replies move in the opposite direction.}
In AITA, $29.3\%$ of blaming and $17.2\%$ of defending minority comments drew
more than one reply, against only $4.6\%$ and $4.1\%$ of their respective
majority baselines. AITAH repeats the asymmetry: $22.7\%$ and $12.8\%$ for
minority comments against $4.1\%$ for both majority baselines. The baselines
are the informative part here. Majority comments are answered at an almost
constant low rate---between $4.1\%$ and $4.6\%$ regardless of post type or
community---while the minority rate varies by a factor of three across the same
cells. Whatever governs how much reply a comment attracts operates almost
entirely on dissent.

\paragraph{Blaming draws more discussion than defending.}
Within the minority, the blaming direction is consistently the more engaged
one. It exceeds defending dissent by a factor of $1.70$ in AITA and $1.77$ in
AITAH on the reply measure, and by $1.87$ and $1.83$ on reply depth, where
$17.8\%$ of blaming against $9.5\%$ of defending comments in AITA, and
$13.2\%$ against $7.2\%$ in AITAH, seed subtrees deeper than two levels. The
absolute rates differ by roughly a third between the two communities, yet every
one of these ratios falls between $1.7$ and $1.9$.

Taken together, these descriptive patterns separate approval from
conversational attention. Minority judgments were strongly disfavored in voting
relative to majority judgments, yet were disproportionately likely to initiate
further discussion, and the contrast is sharpest for blaming dissent. Sanction
and attention therefore do not track a single underlying severity: the direction
that is voted down hardest is also the direction that is answered most.

\subsection{RQ4: AITA/AITAH timeline }
\paragraph{Certainty change over time 18h rule.}
Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-time-series} shows the mean certainty expressed by
majority- and minority-side comments during the first 25 hours after
submission. Majority-side comments express greater certainty than
minority-side comments throughout, in both communities.

AITA shows a significant increase in overall comment certainty after the 18-hour flair lock, from 3.730 to 3.742 (\(p=2.68\times10^{-8}\)). In contrast, AITAH shows no significant pre–post change (\(p=0.111\)). Minority certainty continues to increase beyond the 18-hour mark in AITA, whereas it declines in AITAH. This contrasting trajectory suggests that the verdict flair may act as an incentive for minority commenters to continue participating and express their dissent more assertively. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that the visible flair acts as a social signal that encourages minority commenters to express their disagreement with greater certainty.

Figure~\ref{fig:score-distribution-yana-ge20} presents the post-weighted distributions of median comment scores for majority- and minority-side judgments in matched AITA and AITAH posts. In both communities, majority-side comments generally receive positive scores, whereas minority-side comments generally receive negative scores. This consistent separation indicates that judgments aligned with the prevailing verdict are rewarded, while dissenting judgments are penalised.

The majority--minority score gap is more pronounced in AITA. AITA majority comments attain higher positive scores than their AITAH counterparts, while AITA minority comments receive more strongly negative scores. In AITAH, the same directional pattern remains, but both the reward for majority agreement and the penalty for minority disagreement are less extreme. Overall, the results suggest that minority judgments face a systematic score disadvantage in both communities, with stronger score polarisation in AITA.

\paragraph{Defending-minority judgments become more prevalent in AITA.}
Figure~\ref{fig:minority-ratio-matched} compares the post-level minority-verdict ratio over the first 24 hours in 103 matched AITA--AITAH post pairs. In NA-dominant posts, where YA judgments constitute the minority, the two communities exhibit nearly identical and stable trajectories. The minority ratio remains approximately 9--10\% throughout the observation period, indicating little temporal change in the participation of commenters who blame an OP whom the majority absolves.

A different pattern emerges in YA-dominant posts, where NA judgments constitute the minority. In AITAH, the minority ratio begins at approximately 24--25\% and declines to around 19\% during the first 10--12 hours, after which it remains relatively stable. In AITA, it begins at approximately 21--22\% and gradually increases to about 23\%. Consequently, the communities initially converge but subsequently diverge: over time, defending-minority judgments become relatively more prevalent in AITA and less prevalent in AITAH. The figure therefore suggests that temporal differences between the communities are concentrated among commenters who defend an OP condemned by the majority, rather than among those who blame an OP absolved by the majority. Because the uncertainty intervals overlap substantially, this divergence should be treated as descriptive unless confirmed by an inferential time-by-community interaction test.
