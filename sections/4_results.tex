\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We examine minority reception across agreement levels (RQ1), then compare
blaming and defending dissent in reception, expressed certainty, and reply
orientation (RQ2). We then examine the additional perspectives introduced
in replies (RQ3). AITA is the primary setting; AITAH provides an additional
community comparison.

\subsection{Minority Reception by Agreement Level}
\label{subsec:res-baseline}
\label{subsec:res-distribution}

The discussion environment is predominantly high-agreement: such posts
account for $78.2\%$ of the analyzed AITA posts, and $80.6\%$ of these have a
majority that defends the OP. AITAH exhibits a similar concentration
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-verdict-distribution}). We examine how minority
judgments are received in these discussions, using low-agreement posts
as a comparison.

\paragraph{Lower scores coexist with greater reply engagement.}
In high-agreement posts, minority comments receive lower scores but more
replies and deeper reply threads than majority comments
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). These differences are smaller under
low agreement: the majority--minority mean score gap is approximately
$50$ points under high agreement versus $8$ under low agreement, and
the gaps in reply count and depth also narrow. Thus, stronger agreement
coincides with a wider separation between evaluation and reply engagement.
Figure~\ref{fig:aita-agreement-gradient} in the Appendix shows reception
across the full agreement range, complementing the high--low comparison.
AITAH shows the same high-agreement pattern of lower minority scores,
more replies, and deeper reply trees, with smaller reply-engagement gaps
under low agreement (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aitah-reception}).
These descriptive comparisons establish how reception varies with the
agreement context; they do not estimate an effect of agreement strength.
% Posts without minority comments do not contribute a minority value (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-dimensions}).
Most observed top-level comments arrive within the first day, whereas
nested replies extend further (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:timing}).
Additional analyses
show that early minority comments receive more replies than later ones,
so arrival timing and exposure remain relevant to these aggregate outcomes
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aita-arrival}).

Together, these results address \textbf{RQ1}: minority judgments receive
lower scores but more replies and deeper reply trees, with larger
majority--minority gaps under high agreement.

\subsection{Blaming Versus Defending Dissent}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

\paragraph{Blaming dissent receives more replies and deeper reply trees.}
Both directions receive negative mean scores: $-10.65$ for blaming dissent
in posts with a defending majority and $-8.62$ for defending dissent in
posts with a blaming majority (Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). The
pooled majority reference is $39.55$. Blaming and defending partition the
high-agreement minority, rather than representing additional agreement levels.

The more pronounced directional contrast is in engagement. Blaming dissent
averages $0.80$ direct replies versus $0.44$ for defending dissent, a
$1.83$-fold difference calculated from unrounded post-equal means.
Maximum reply depths average $1.12$ versus $0.70$, a $1.60$-fold difference.
The corresponding majority baselines are similar across the two post
contexts: $0.15$ and $0.16$ replies, with depths of $0.17$ in both.
The same directional ordering appears in AITAH: blaming dissent receives
lower scores, more replies, and deeper reply trees than defending dissent
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aitah-reception}).
These comparisons involve different sets of stories and potentially different
contributing post sets, rather than judgments randomly assigned to the
same story. They do not establish why blaming dissent receives more engagement.

\paragraph{Both minority directions end with higher expressed certainty.}
\label{subsec:res-certainty-contrasts}
Figure~\ref{fig:aita-certainty-progress} shows mean certainty across ten
approximately equal-count stages of each post's first-day verdict-comment
sequence in high-agreement posts.
Majority certainty remains comparatively high, with a small positive trend.
Both blaming and defending minority comments have higher mean certainty
at the final stage than at the initial stage, with fluctuations between
stages. Neither minority profile increases monotonically.

Using post fixed-effects regression across all ten stages, the estimated
change from the first to the last stage is $0.014$ for majority, $0.100$
for blaming, and $0.119$ for defending comments. These estimates summarize
linear trends rather than the observed difference between the two endpoints.
Majority and blaming trends remain significant after Holm
correction ($p<.001$); defending does not ($p=.066$). Each profile uses a separate fixed
set of posts with scored comments of that group in every stage. These tests
do not establish differences between group slopes or monotonic increases.
Successive comments may come from different participants, so the trends
do not establish increased certainty within individuals.

Full-period distributions, sensitivity checks, and corresponding AITAH trends
appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty}. The full-period directional
contrast is sensitive to annotation version and should not be interpreted
as evidence of different first-day trajectories.

\paragraph{Support predominates for majority judgments, challenge for minority judgments.}
\label{subsec:res-rq2}
Among annotated replies in high-agreement AITA posts, approximately
$68.7\%$ support majority comments and $21.8\%$ challenge them
(Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}). For blaming minority comments,
$16.1\%$ of replies support and $77.3\%$ challenge; for defending minority
comments, the corresponding proportions are $18.6\%$ and $73.7\%$.
Information-seeking and other replies account for the remaining shares.
Both minority directions therefore predominantly receive challenges
in the annotated sample.

The difference between the minority directions is smaller than their
contrast with the majority: blaming has a $3.6$-percentage-point higher
challenge proportion and a $2.5$-percentage-point lower support proportion
than defending, based on the rounded values. These descriptive differences
do not establish a statistically reliable directional difference in reply
composition.

These pair-weighted proportions describe the first annotation sample,
not the probability that a comment receives a challenge. The sample covers
only high-agreement posts and differs from the reception dataset; it
therefore establishes neither a high--low difference in challenge
proportions nor the composition of the additional replies associated
with blaming dissent in the reception analysis.

Together, these findings address \textbf{RQ2}: blaming dissent receives
more replies and deeper reply trees than defending dissent, while both
minority directions predominantly receive challenges. Both show higher
mean expressed certainty at the end of the first-day sequence, without
establishing different rates of increase between them.
