In this section, we examine minority reception by agreement level and, within high-agreement posts, by the blaming or defending direction of dissent (RQ1).
Building on this, we explore reply stance and the additional moral
reasons available in comment--reply exchanges, with comment certainty (RQ2).

High-agreement posts account for 78.2\% of the analyzed AITA posts. 
Among these high-agreement posts, 80.6\% have an NA majority, 
meaning that the prevailing judgment defends the OP (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-evidence}). 
We examine how minority
judgments are received in these discussions, using low-agreement posts
as a comparison.

\runinheading{Lower scores, more replies, deeper threads.}
%\runinheading{Lower scores coexist with greater reply engagement.}
In high-agreement posts, minority comments receive lower scores but more
replies and deeper reply threads than majority comments
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). 
On average, minority comments receive $50$ points lower than majority (versus a difference of $8$ under high agreement), receive about $4.9$ times as many replies, and have a mean maximum reply depth about $6.1$ times that of majority comments.

Within high-agreement discussions, we next compare blaming and defending dissent. Blaming dissent receives more replies on average (0.80 versus 0.44) and has deeper reply threads (mean maximum depth 1.12 versus 0.70; Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). Both differences are significant after Holm correction ($p<.001$). Mean scores are negative for both directions; blaming dissent has a lower observed mean, but this difference is not significant after correction ($p=.057$). 
The same engagement pattern recurs in AITAH (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview}).

For \textbf{RQ1}, minority comments receive lower scores but more
replies and deeper threads, with larger majority--minority gaps under
high agreement. Davis and Graham similarly report greater engagement
with downvoted comments \cite{davis2021emotional}. Our analysis identify minority comments as containing a less common judgment (YA or NA); different than vote
scores and emotional tone\cite{davis2021emotional}. Greater
engagement with these judgments may reflect attempts to contest dissent rather than endorse it \cite{schachter1951deviation}. Within
high-agreement discussions, blaming dissent attracts more extensive
discussion than defending dissent. This may reflect the scrutiny and
demands for justification that public blame can invite
\cite{malle2022socially}, although comparisons across different post sets
do not establish this mechanism.

\subsection{Minority Expression and Discussion}
\label{subsec:res-perspectives}

We turn from how much discussion minority judgments attract to what
those exchanges contain (\textbf{RQ2}): reply stance and additional moral
reasons. We also compare how firmly majority and minority judgments are
expressed.

\runinheading{Support for the majority, challenge for the minority.}
\label{subsec:res-reply-orientation}
Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}a compares reply stance across judgment
groups. Replies predominantly support majority comments (68.7\%) and $21.8\%$ challenge them.
For blaming minority comments, 77.3\% of replies express challenge and
16.1\% express support; for defending minority comments, the corresponding
proportions are 73.7\% and 18.6\%. A combined ``other'' category is made up of Information-seeking and other replies.

\runinheading{Challenges more often add moral reasons.}
%\runinheading{Challenges to defending dissent most often add moral reasons.}
Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}b compares how often replies add moral
reasons absent from their parent comments. Overall, addition rates are
69.1\% for replies to defending dissent, 55.8\% for blaming dissent, and
48.2\% for majority comments; only the defending--majority difference is
significant after Holm correction ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.0024$).
Challenging replies add reasons more often than supportive replies
within all three groups (all $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.01$).
Challenges to defending dissent have the highest addition rate (83.4\%),
exceeding challenges to both majority and blaming minority comments
(both $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.01$). Among supportive replies, however,
blaming dissent has a lower addition rate than majority comments
(21.4\% versus 46.3\%; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.0076$).

\runinheading{Certainty across discussion stages.}
\label{subsec:res-certainty-contrasts}
Fig.~\ref{fig:aita-certainty-progress} shows mean expressed certainty
across ten chronological stages within the first 24 hours in high-agreement
AITA posts (left) and the estimated linear slope across these stages
(right). Overall, majority comments express higher certainty than either
minority group, and blaming dissent is consistently more certain than
defending dissent.
Blaming dissent also has a higher post-averaged share of maximally
certain comments than defending dissent ($66.1\%$ versus $58.1\%$;
Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high}).
Across stages, mean certainty increases for blaming dissent, while
majority certainty remains high with only a small increase. 
For each group, we estimate a linear trend across all ten stages,
using the same set of posts throughout and accounting for differences
between posts. Blaming dissent shows a statistically significant
increase in expressed certainty ($\hat{\beta}=0.100$,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.001$).
Sensitivity checks and AITAH results are summarized in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-evidence}.

For \textbf{RQ2}, minority judgments are predominantly challenged,
yet these exchanges can introduce additional moral reasons. Challenges
add reasons more often than support across all three judgment groups.
Our analysis extends work on moral reasoning within AITA comments
\cite{xi2024morality} by examining how reasons develop through
comment--reply exchanges. This connects with research linking
disagreement to a broader repertoire of reasons
\cite{price2002disagreement}, while identifying reasons available in
discussion rather than demonstrating changes in readers' understanding.
Certainty provides a complementary distinction: blaming dissent is
expressed more firmly than defending dissent. Alongside research
linking doubtful language to lower comment popularity
\cite{evans2023doubt}, these findings show how expressed certainty varies
with the direction of moral opposition. Together, the results distinguish
acceptance of a judgment from the additional reasons developed in
responding to it.
