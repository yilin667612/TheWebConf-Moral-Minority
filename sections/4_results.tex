\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We examine minority reception by agreement level and, within high-agreement
posts, by the blaming or defending direction of dissent (RQ1). Expressed
certainty further characterizes these directional patterns. We then examine
reply stance and the additional moral reasons introduced
within supportive and challenging replies (RQ2). AITA is the primary setting; AITAH provides an additional
community comparison.

\subsection{Minority Reception Across Agreement Levels and Moral Directions}
\label{subsec:res-reception}

\subsubsection{High- Versus Low-Agreement Discussions}
\label{subsec:res-baseline}
\label{subsec:res-distribution}

The discussion environment is predominantly high-agreement: such posts
account for $78.2\%$ of the analyzed AITA posts, and $80.6\%$ of these have a
majority that defends the OP. AITAH exhibits a similar concentration
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-evidence}). We examine how minority
judgments are received in these discussions, using low-agreement posts
as a comparison.

\runinheading{Lower scores coexist with greater reply engagement.}
In high-agreement posts, minority comments receive lower scores but more
replies and deeper reply threads than majority comments
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). These differences are smaller under
low agreement: the majority--minority mean score gap is approximately
$50$ points under high agreement versus $8$ under low agreement, and
the gaps in reply count and depth also narrow.
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aita-agreement-gradient} shows reception
across the full agreement range, complementing the high--low comparison.
The same reception pattern recurs in AITAH
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview}).

\runinheading{Higher reply engagement extends to later-arriving minority comments.}
High-agreement minority comments posted later still receive more replies
on average than majority comments posted in the same hourly bins
(Figure~\ref{fig:comment-engagement-over-time}). Although mean reply counts
decline from early to late arrival hours in all three groups, the
minority--majority gap persists throughout the first 24 hours. Greater
engagement with minority judgments is therefore not limited to the
earliest comments.

Scores follow different arrival-time patterns. High-agreement majority
comments and low-agreement comments pooled across alignments have high
initial mean scores that approach one for later arrivals. Minority mean
scores are negative in the early hours and move toward zero or slightly
positive values for later arrivals.

These profiles average recorded reception within each post, hour, and
judgment group, then weight contributing posts equally. Post sets vary
by hour, and percentile bands show variation across post means. The
curves compare comments arriving at different times, not how long
individual comments continue receiving replies. Aggregation details
appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-annotation-details}.

\subsubsection{Blaming Versus Defending Dissent in High-Agreement Discussions}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

Within high-agreement discussions, we distinguish blaming from defending
dissent to examine how reception and expression vary with the direction
of opposition to the majority.

\runinheading{Blaming dissent receives more replies and deeper reply trees.}
The clearest directional contrast is in engagement: blaming dissent
averages $0.80$ replies versus $0.44$ for defending dissent, with mean
maximum reply depths of $1.12$ versus $0.70$
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). Both contrasts remain significant
after Holm correction ($p<.001$), while majority engagement is similar
across the two post contexts. Both minority directions receive negative
mean scores; blaming's observed mean is lower, but this contrast does not
reach the $.05$ threshold after correction ($p=.057$).
The directional ordering also appears in AITAH
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview}). These contrasts
compare blaming and defending dissent across their respective
high-agreement post sets.

\runinheading{Certainty and discussion progress.}
\label{subsec:res-certainty-contrasts}
Across the first-day comment sequence, majority certainty remains
comparatively high, and both minority profiles end above their initial
means despite fluctuations between stages
(Figure~\ref{fig:aita-certainty-progress}).

Using post fixed-effects regression across all ten stages, the estimated
linear change from the first to the last stage is $0.014$ for majority,
$0.100$ for blaming, and $0.119$ for defending comments.
Majority and blaming trends remain significant after Holm
correction ($p<.001$); defending does not ($p=.066$). These trends describe
successive comments within a fixed post cohort for each group.
Sensitivity checks and AITAH results are summarized in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-evidence}.

Over the full observed period, blaming dissent also has a higher
post-averaged proportion of maximally certain comments than defending
dissent in the annotated high-agreement AITA sample (an
$8.0$-percentage-point gap; Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high}).
This contrast is sensitive to annotation version and post composition
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-evidence}).

For \textbf{RQ1}, reception varies with both agreement level and the
moral direction of dissent. Majority--minority gaps in scores, reply
counts, and depth are larger under high than low agreement; within
high-agreement discussions, blaming dissent attracts more and deeper
discussion than defending dissent.
Blaming dissent also shows a higher share of maximally certain comments
and a positive certainty trend across the first-day sequence. The
reception contrast recurs in both communities; the certainty estimates
describe the annotated post sets.

\subsection{Reply Stance and Additional Moral Reasons}
\label{subsec:res-perspectives}

We first compare how replies respond to majority, blaming minority, and
defending minority judgments, then examine how often supportive and
challenging replies add moral reasons beyond their parent comment.
% Reply stance and the corresponding moral reason are annotated separately; addition does not require disagreement.

\runinheading{Support for the majority, challenge for the minority.}
\label{subsec:res-reply-orientation}
Annotated replies predominantly support majority comments but challenge
both minority directions (Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}a). In AITA,
$68.7\%$ support majority comments and $21.8\%$ challenge them;
for blaming minority comments,
$16.1\%$ of replies support and $77.3\%$ challenge; for defending minority
comments, the corresponding proportions are $18.6\%$ and $73.7\%$.
Information-seeking and other replies account for the remaining shares.

The majority--minority contrast in reply composition is much larger than
the difference between blaming and defending minority comments.
These pair-weighted proportions come from the high-agreement
reply-annotation sample mentioned in Section~\ref{subsec:method-annotation}.

\runinheading{Replies to minority judgments more often add moral reasons.}
Across 7,023 direct reply pairs from 100 high-agreement AITA posts,
post-equal mean addition rates are 48.2\% for replies to majority
comments, 55.8\% for replies to blaming minority comments, and 69.1\%
for replies to defending minority comments
(Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}b). Thus, both minority groups have
higher observed overall rates than the majority group, with defending
minority comments having the highest mean. After Holm correction across
12 exploratory comparisons, the defending minority--majority contrast
is significant ($\Delta=20.9$ percentage points, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.0024$),
whereas the blaming minority--majority contrast is not
($\Delta=7.6$ points, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.173$). The difference between
the two minority groups is also not significant after correction
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.113$). An addition denotes a moral
consideration recorded in the reply but absent from its parent's recorded
perspective set; it need not be new to the discussion as a whole.

\runinheading{Challenges add moral reasons more often than support.}
Within each parent group, challenging replies have higher observed
addition rates than supportive replies: 62.5\% versus 46.3\% for majority
parents, 65.4\% versus 21.4\% for blaming minority parents, and 83.4\%
versus 43.1\% for defending minority parents. All three challenge--support
contrasts are significant after correction (majority:
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.001$; blaming minority: $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.001$;
defending minority: $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.0019$). The overall minority--majority
pattern therefore does not hold uniformly within reply stances.
Supportive replies to both minority groups have lower observed rates
than supportive replies to majority comments, whereas challenging
replies to defending minority comments have the highest observed rate.
Within support, the blaming minority--majority difference is significant
($\Delta=-24.9$ points, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.0076$), but the defending
minority--majority and defending--blaming minority differences are not
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1.000$ and $.157$, respectively). Within challenge,
defending minority replies have significantly higher addition rates
than both majority replies ($\Delta=21.0$ points, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.001$)
and blaming minority replies ($\Delta=18.0$ points,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.0041$); the blaming minority--majority difference
is not significant ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1.000$).
Both support and challenge nevertheless introduce considerations beyond
those expressed by the parent.

For \textbf{RQ2}, replies predominantly support majority judgments and
challenge minority judgments. Both stances can add new moral
reasons, with higher addition rates in challenges within each
parent group. The overall descriptive pattern links minority parents
to more frequent additions, particularly for defending dissent, while
the within-stance comparisons show that this pattern varies with
how replies respond to the parent. Estimates give equal weight to
contributing posts within each group and reply stance, and
Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}b reports $95\%$ post-bootstrap confidence
intervals. The tests above assess contrasts directly, separately from the
figure's intervals around individual means.
