\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We first establish the setting in which minority judgments occur, then
examine their reception across agreement levels (RQ1), distinguish blaming
from defending dissent (RQ2), and assess which patterns recur in AITAH
(RQ3). AITA is the primary setting throughout. The directional comparison
includes both reception and expressed certainty. Cumulative participation
is examined separately; neither linguistic certainty nor participation
is treated as a measure of individual attitude change.

\subsection{Context: Agreement and the Direction of Minority Judgments}
\label{subsec:res-distribution}

High agreement is common in the AITA cohort, rather than an exceptional
condition. Of the $28{,}107$ posts with at least $200$ retained verdicts,
$21{,}978$ ($78.2\%$) fall in the high-agreement bands and $1{,}838$
($6.5\%$) in the low-agreement band after exact-tie exclusion.
Figure~\ref{fig:ya-share} shows a bimodal distribution of final YA share,
with the larger concentration near the NA end. Within the high-agreement
cohort, $80.6\%$ of posts are NA-dominant. Consequently, the more common
setting for dissent is one in which a minority assigns blame to an OP whom
most commenters excuse.

Post frequency and discussion volume are distinct. The red line in
Figure~\ref{fig:ya-share} shows mean retained top-level verdicts per post
within each YA-share bin. Although NA-dominant posts are more numerous,
the average verdict count is higher toward the YA end, particularly near
unanimous YA judgments. The two axes therefore distinguish how often a
judgment context occurs from how many verdict comments its posts attract.

This distribution does not establish a community-wide aversion to blame.
The stories are supplied by the people whose conduct is being judged.
Research on authors' dual roles as characters and narrators
\cite{giorgi2023author}, and on selective retelling and possible
validation-seeking \cite{haworth2021reasonability}, provides context for
the imbalance without identifying the motives of individual posters.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:verdict-distribution} reports the broader
distributional summary.

Early verdict leaders also correspond more closely to the final dominant
side in high-agreement than low-agreement posts
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:consensus-stabilization}). This provides descriptive
context for the final-alignment classification; it does not establish
which comments later participants saw or how they perceived the prevailing
opinion.

\subsection{Minority Reception Across Agreement Levels}
\label{subsec:res-baseline}

\paragraph{Lower scores coexist with greater reply engagement.}
In high-agreement AITA posts, majority comments average $37.95$ score
points, whereas minority comments average $-2.75$
(Figure~\ref{fig:engagement-by-consensus}). The ordering reverses for
conversational engagement: minority comments receive $0.53$ direct replies
on average, compared with $0.14$ for majority comments, and their mean
maximum reply depth is $0.79$ rather than $0.14$. Thus, low evaluation
does not imply an absence of responses.

The differences are smaller in low-agreement posts. Majority and minority
scores average $33.65$ and $26.87$; direct-reply counts average $0.26$ and
$0.30$; and maximum reply depths average $0.30$ and $0.35$, respectively.
The mean score gap is therefore $40.70$ points under high agreement but
$6.78$ under low agreement. These archive-level comparisons establish the
reception baseline for the directional analysis, rather than treating the
coexistence of low scores and more replies as a new phenomenon. They are
descriptive contrasts, not estimates of an effect of agreement strength.

\paragraph{Arrival timing qualifies the aggregate averages.}
Figure~\ref{fig:comment-engagement-over-time} groups comments by their
arrival hour during the first $18$ hours. Early high-agreement majority
comments have higher recorded scores, while early minority comments
receive more direct replies than later minority comments. The separation
between means, medians, and percentile bands also shows why a mean alone
does not describe the reception of a typical comment. These are final
recorded outcomes grouped by arrival, not trajectories of scores or replies
measured repeatedly over a comment's lifetime. Arrival timing and exposure
therefore remain relevant when interpreting the aggregate contrasts.

\paragraph{Attention is not equivalent to support.}
\label{subsec:res-rq2}
The direct-response annotations indicate a higher challenge proportion for
minority than majority parent comments in each analyzed high-agreement
community-by-verdict-tail stratum. OP-authored pairs are excluded, so this
pattern concerns responses from other commenters rather than only the
person being judged. Together with the structural results, it suggests
that minority judgments attract both attention and contestation.

This comparison concerns the composition of sampled direct replies,
conditional on a reply being present and eligible for annotation. It does
not estimate the probability that a minority comment receives a challenge.
Moreover, the response samples cover high-agreement posts only and use a
broader sampling frame than the $200$-verdict reception cohort; they do not
establish a high--low agreement difference in challenge rates.

Taken together, these results address \textbf{RQ1}: minority judgments
combine lower evaluation with greater reply engagement, with larger
majority--minority gaps under high agreement. The annotated high-agreement
replies further indicate that attention to minority judgments includes
more contestation, not simply support.

\subsection{Blaming Versus Defending Dissent}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

\paragraph{Both directions are negatively evaluated, but engagement differs.}
Separating high-agreement minority comments by dissent direction reveals
an asymmetry that the pooled minority category conceals
(Figure~\ref{fig:dissent-direction}). Blaming comments in NA-dominant posts
average $-2.92$ score points, compared with $37.73$ for their majority
reference. Defending comments in YA-dominant posts average $-2.36$,
compared with $38.54$ for their majority reference. Both minority directions
therefore receive negative mean scores, with only a modest difference
between their means.

The clearer directional difference is in conversational engagement.
Blaming comments average $0.63$ direct replies, compared with $0.32$ for
defending comments---a $1.98$-fold difference calculated from unrounded
means. Their mean maximum reply depths are $0.92$ and $0.49$, respectively,
a $1.86$-fold difference. In contrast, the corresponding majority baselines
are similar: $0.14$ direct replies in both post types, and depths of $0.14$
and $0.12$. Challenging collective defense of the OP is thus associated
with more replies and deeper reply trees than challenging collective blame.

These comparisons use different story cohorts, not alternative judgments
randomly assigned to the same story. They do not establish why blaming
dissent attracts more engagement, nor whether its additional replies are
supportive or challenging. In particular, the majority--minority challenge
comparison above cannot substitute for a direct blaming--defending
response-type comparison.

\paragraph{Expressed certainty differs by dissent direction.}
\label{subsec:res-certainty-contrasts}
The directional comparison also examines how firmly blaming and defending
judgments are expressed. In high-agreement AITA posts, the mean within-post
proportion of minority comments assigned certainty level $4$ is $66.1\%$
for blaming and $58.1\%$ for defending: a difference of $8.0$ percentage
points ($95\%$ whole-post bootstrap CI $[3.9,12.2]$).
Under low agreement, the contrast is smaller and uncertain
($1.2$ percentage points; CI $[-1.6,4.1]$).
These estimates weight posts equally, unlike the pooled-comment reception
means, and use the full observed annotation period.

The contrast requires qualification: restricting high-agreement AITA to
annotation version \texttt{v4} yields a positive estimate whose interval
includes zero. Version and post composition cannot be separated in that
comparison. The result characterizes expressed language, not private
confidence or certainty increasing over time; nor do the separate
intervals establish an agreement-by-direction interaction.
Full distributions and sensitivity checks appear in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty-comparison}.

Together, these findings address \textbf{RQ2} through both reception and
expression: blaming dissent attracts more replies and deeper reply trees
than defending dissent, while the annotated high-agreement AITA sample
also shows a higher proportion of firmly asserted judgments. The certainty
contrast remains subject to the annotation-version and validation
qualifications above.

\subsection{Reception Patterns Across Communities}
\label{subsec:res-community}

\paragraph{The agreement-level pattern recurs.}
AITAH also combines lower minority scores with greater engagement under
high agreement (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-engagement-full}).
Majority and minority mean scores are $14.30$ and $-0.19$, respectively;
direct-reply counts are $0.12$ and $0.38$; and maximum reply depths are
$0.12$ and $0.67$. Under low agreement, the reply-count difference nearly
disappears: both means round to $0.21$, with depths of $0.32$ and $0.34$.
As in AITA, the high-agreement minority therefore occupies a distinctive
combination of lower evaluation and greater conversational engagement.

\paragraph{Directional ordering is shared, not its magnitude.}
Blaming dissent in AITAH receives a lower mean score than defending dissent
($-0.42$ versus $1.03$), more direct replies ($0.41$ versus $0.23$), and
greater maximum reply depth ($0.72$ versus $0.41$)
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-dissent-direction}).
The blaming-to-defending reply ratio is $1.80$, compared with $1.98$ in
AITA. AITAH's defending comments retain a positive mean score, whereas
both directions have negative means in AITA. These results show a shared
directional ordering alongside differences in recorded magnitudes.

Raw cross-community differences do not, by themselves, establish greater
tolerance, stronger normative pressure, or an effect of AITA's verdict
rule. The comparisons retain their analysis-specific observation frames
and may differ in audience, story composition, and exposure.
The AITAH certainty estimates are also limited by only $15$
high-agreement defending posts
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high}).

not imply equivalent reception environments or identify their causes.

\subsection{Supplementary Analysis: Cumulative Minority Participation}
\label{subsec:res-temporal}

Reception and participation describe different aspects of dissent.
Figure~\ref{fig:minority-ratio-time} supplements the reception analyses with
cumulative minority shares in high-agreement posts during the first $24$
hours. In AITA, both directions initially decline. Blaming share then
recovers modestly and levels off, while defending share rises more
gradually, ending close to its initial level. In AITAH, blaming share
increases modestly, whereas defending share declines sharply in the early
hours and subsequently changes little. The directional reception ordering
therefore coexists with different descriptive participation patterns.

    Blaming dissent occurs in NA-dominant posts; defending dissent occurs

    \label{fig:minority-ratio-time}

These curves pool verdict comments up to each checkpoint; they are neither
hour-specific shares nor averages giving each post equal weight.
Cumulative aggregation retains earlier observations and can make later
changes appear small. Accordingly, the curves do not establish stable
window-specific participation, a regime change, or withdrawal by
particular individuals. They also cover only the first day, not the full
observed discussion period, and should not be conflated with the
annotation-sample comment-sequence analyses in
Section~\ref{subsec:method-temporal}.
