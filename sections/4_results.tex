\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We examine minority reception across agreement levels (RQ1), then compare
blaming and defending dissent in reception, expressed certainty, and reply
orientation (RQ2). We then examine the additional perspectives introduced
in replies (RQ3). AITA is the primary setting; AITAH provides an additional
community comparison.

\subsection{Minority Reception by Agreement Level}
\label{subsec:res-baseline}
\label{subsec:res-distribution}

The discussion environment is predominantly high-agreement: such posts
account for $78.2\%$ of the analyzed AITA posts, and $80.6\%$ of these have a
majority that defends the OP. AITAH exhibits a similar concentration
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-verdict-distribution}). We examine how minority
judgments are received in these discussions, using low-agreement posts
as a comparison.

\paragraph{Lower scores coexist with greater reply engagement.}
In high-agreement posts, minority comments receive lower scores but more
replies and deeper reply threads than majority comments
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). These differences are smaller under
low agreement: the majority--minority mean score gap is approximately
$50$ points under high agreement versus $8$ under low agreement, and
the gaps in reply count and depth also narrow.
Figure~\ref{fig:aita-agreement-gradient} in the Appendix shows reception
across the full agreement range, complementing the high--low comparison.
The same reception pattern recurs in AITAH
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aitah-reception}).

Figure~\ref{fig:comment-engagement-over-time} situates reception within
discussion progress. We order each post's first-day judgment comments
and divide them into ten approximately equal-count stages before splitting
alignment. Each group uses a fixed set of posts represented in every stage,
with equal weight per post. Minority comments appearing earlier receive
more replies than later ones; early majority comments have higher recorded
scores. These are archive-wide outcomes for different comments, not changes
in the same comment's reception. Arrival timing and exposure remain relevant
to the aggregate comparisons; aggregation and selection details appear in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aita-arrival}.

For \textbf{RQ1}, high agreement is associated with larger
majority--minority reception gaps; these descriptive comparisons do not
identify a causal effect of agreement strength.

\subsection{Blaming Versus Defending Dissent}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

Within high-agreement discussions, we distinguish blaming from defending
dissent to examine how reception and expression vary with the direction
of opposition to the majority.

\paragraph{Blaming dissent receives more replies and deeper reply trees.}
The clearest directional contrast is in engagement: blaming dissent
averages $0.80$ replies versus $0.44$ for defending dissent, with mean
maximum reply depths of $1.12$ versus $0.70$
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). Both contrasts remain significant
after Holm correction ($p<.001$), while majority engagement is similar
across the two post contexts. Both minority directions receive negative
mean scores; blaming's observed mean is lower, but this contrast does not
reach the $.05$ threshold after correction ($p=.057$).
The directional ordering also appears in AITAH
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aitah-reception}). Because the directions
occur in different stories and contributing post sets can differ, these
comparisons do not identify why blaming dissent receives more engagement.

\paragraph{Expressed certainty and discussion progress.}
\label{subsec:res-certainty-contrasts}
Over the full observed period, blaming dissent has a higher post-averaged
proportion of maximally certain comments than defending dissent in the
annotated high-agreement AITA sample (an $8.0$-percentage-point gap).
This contrast is sensitive to annotation version and post composition
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty}).

We also examine expression as discussions progress.
Figure~\ref{fig:aita-certainty-progress} shows mean certainty across ten
approximately equal-count stages of each post's first-day judgment-comment
sequence. Majority certainty remains comparatively high. Both minority
profiles end above their initial means, but fluctuate between stages.

Using post fixed-effects regression across all ten stages, the estimated
change from the first to the last stage is $0.014$ for majority, $0.100$
for blaming, and $0.119$ for defending comments. These estimates summarize
linear trends rather than the observed difference between the two endpoints.
Majority and blaming trends remain significant after Holm
correction ($p<.001$); defending does not ($p=.066$). Each profile uses a
separate fixed set of posts with annotated comments of that group in every
stage. Neither these separate trend tests nor the full-period contrast
establish different trajectories between the directions. Successive
comments may come from different participants, so the profiles do not
demonstrate within-person increases. Sensitivity checks and corresponding
AITAH trends appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty}.

\paragraph{Support predominates for majority judgments, challenge for minority judgments.}
\label{subsec:res-rq2}
Annotated replies predominantly support majority comments but challenge
both minority directions (Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}). In AITA,
$68.7\%$ support majority comments and $21.8\%$ challenge them;
for blaming minority comments,
$16.1\%$ of replies support and $77.3\%$ challenge; for defending minority
comments, the corresponding proportions are $18.6\%$ and $73.7\%$.
Information-seeking and other replies account for the remaining shares.

The observed blaming--defending differences are small relative to the
majority--minority contrast and have not been established as statistically
reliable. These pair-weighted proportions describe reply composition in
the first high-agreement annotation sample, not a comment's probability
of receiving a challenge. Because this sample is separate from the
reception data and excludes low-agreement posts, it identifies neither
the composition of blaming dissent's additional replies nor a high--low
difference in challenge proportions.

Together, these findings address \textbf{RQ2}: blaming dissent receives
more replies and deeper reply trees than defending dissent, while both
minority directions predominantly receive challenges. The annotated
sample suggests a directional difference in expressed certainty, but
its sensitivity and the separate trend estimates do not establish
distinct developmental trajectories.
