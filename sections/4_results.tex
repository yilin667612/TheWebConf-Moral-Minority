\subsection{Minority Reception Across Agreement Levels and Moral Directions}
\label{subsec:res-reception}

\subsubsection{Majority Versus Minority Reception Under High and Low Agreement}
\label{subsec:res-baseline}

High-agreement posts account for 78.2\% of the analyzed AITA posts. 
Among these high-agreement posts, 80.6\% have an NA majority, 
meaning that the prevailing judgment defends the OP (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-evidence}). 
We examine how minority
judgments are received in these discussions, using low-agreement posts
as a comparison.

\runinheading{Lower scores coexist with greater reply engagement.}
In high-agreement posts, minority comments receive lower scores but more
replies and deeper reply threads than majority comments
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). 
On average, minority comments receive about $4.9$ times as many replies and have a mean maximum reply depth about $6.1$ times that of majority comments.
Under low agreement, 
majority--minority gaps narrow in mean score 
(approximately $8$ versus $50$ points under high agreement), reply count, and depth.
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aita-agreement-gradient} shows reception
across the full agreement range, complementing the high--low comparison.

\runinheading{Minority comments attract more replies across arrival hours.}
Mean scores are less extreme for later arrivals, consistent with less
time to accumulate votes (Figure~\ref{fig:comment-engagement-over-time}).
Mean scores for high-agreement majority comments and low-agreement
comments pooled across alignments fall steeply toward one, whereas
minority means shift more gradually from negative values toward zero
or slightly positive values.

Mean reply counts decline across arrival hours in all three groups. 
Even among later arrivals, minority comments in high-agreement posts receive more replies on average than majority comments in the same hourly bins (Figure~\ref{fig:comment-engagement-over-time}). 

These profiles compare comments by arrival hour, with percentile bands
showing variation across post means. The same reception pattern recurs in AITAH
(Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview} and \ref{fig:aitah-arrival-reception}).


\subsubsection{Blaming Versus Defending Dissent in High-Agreement Discussions}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

Within high-agreement discussions, we distinguish blaming from defending
dissent to examine how reception varies with the direction
of opposition to the majority.

\runinheading{Blaming dissent receives more replies and deeper reply trees.}
The clearest directional contrast is in engagement: blaming dissent
averages $0.80$ replies versus $0.44$ for defending dissent, with mean
maximum reply depths of $1.12$ versus $0.70$
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). Both contrasts remain significant
after Holm correction ($p<.001$).
Both minority directions receive negative mean scores. 
Blaming dissent has a lower observed mean than defending dissent, but the difference is not significant after Holm correction ($p=.057$).
The directional ordering also appears in AITAH
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview}). 

For \textbf{RQ1}, minority comments receive lower scores but more replies and deeper reply threads than majority comments, with larger differences under high than low agreement. 
Within high-agreement discussions, blaming dissent receives more replies and has deeper reply threads than defending dissent.


\subsection{Minority Expression and Discussion}
\label{subsec:res-perspectives}

Minority judgments attract more replies and deeper threads, motivating closer examination of how dissent is expressed and how others respond (\textbf{RQ2}). We compare expressed certainty across majority, blaming minority, and defending minority comments, then examine reply stance and whether supportive and challenging replies introduce moral reasons absent from their parent comments.

\input{components/fig_reply_results}

\runinheading{Certainty and discussion progress.}
\label{subsec:res-certainty-contrasts}
Across the first-day comment sequence in high-agreement AITA posts,
both minority groups show lower mean expressed certainty than the
majority, with blaming dissent consistently above defending dissent
(Figure~\ref{fig:aita-certainty-progress}). 
Blaming dissent also has a higher post-averaged share of maximally
certain comments than defending dissent ($66.1\%$ versus $58.1\%$;
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high}).

Notably, blaming dissent also shows increasing expressed certainty as
the first-day comment sequence progresses. Post fixed-effects regression
using all ten stages within a fixed post cohort for each group estimates
a linear increase of $0.100$ from the first to the last stage
($p<.001$, Holm-adjusted). Majority certainty remains high and nearly
stable (estimated increase $0.014$; $p<.001$), while defending dissent's
estimated increase of $0.119$ does not remain significant after Holm
correction ($p=.066$).
Sensitivity checks and AITAH results are summarized in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-evidence}.



\runinheading{Support for the majority, challenge for the minority.}
\label{subsec:res-reply-orientation}
Annotated replies predominantly support majority comments but challenge
both minority directions (Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}a). In AITA,
$68.7\%$ support majority comments and $21.8\%$ challenge them;
for blaming minority comments,
$16.1\%$ of replies support and $77.3\%$ challenge; for defending minority
comments, the corresponding proportions are $18.6\%$ and $73.7\%$.
Information-seeking and other replies account for the remaining shares.
% The majority--minority contrast in reply composition is much larger than
% the difference between blaming and defending minority comments.
% These pair-weighted proportions come from the high-agreement
% reply-annotation sample mentioned in Section~\ref{subsec:method-annotation}.

\runinheading{Challenges to defending dissent most often add moral reasons.}
Across 100 high-agreement AITA posts, replies to both blaming and
defending minority parents show higher observed overall mean addition
rates than replies to majority parents (Figure~\ref{fig:reply-responses}b).
Replies to defending dissent have the highest observed rate ($69.1\%$ versus $48.2\%$ for majority comments), 
and the defending--majority contrast is the only overall group comparison significant after Holm correction ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.0024$).
Challenging replies add moral reasons more often than supportive replies
in all three parent groups (all $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.01$).
Challenges to defending dissent have the highest observed rate ($83.4\%$), 
exceeding challenges to both majority and blaming minority comments after Holm correction (both $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.01$).
Supportive replies to blaming dissent have a lower observed addition
rate than supportive replies to majority comments ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.0076$). 
These findings show
how replies can dispute a minority judgment while introducing moral
reasons beyond those expressed by the parent.

For \textbf{RQ2}, replies to both minority groups have higher observed overall rates of adding moral reasons than replies to majority comments, with the defending–majority contrast significant after Holm correction.
Replies predominantly support majority
judgments and challenge minority judgments. 
Challenging replies introduce moral reasons absent from the parent comment more often than supportive replies in all three parent groups. The highest observed addition rate occurs in challenges to defending dissent.
