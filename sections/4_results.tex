\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We examine minority reception across agreement levels (RQ1), then compare
blaming and defending dissent in reception, reply orientation, and expressed
certainty (RQ2). Sections on perspectives in replies (RQ3) and survey
responses (RQ4) reserve space for the additional results. AITA is the
primary setting; AITAH provides a supplementary community comparison.

\subsection{Minority Reception Across Agreement Levels}
\label{subsec:res-baseline}
\label{subsec:res-distribution}

High agreement is common in the AITA cohort: of $28{,}107$ posts with at
least $200$ retained verdicts, $21{,}978$ ($78.2\%$) meet the high-agreement
threshold and $1{,}838$ ($6.5\%$) meet the low-agreement threshold, excluding
exact ties. Among high-agreement posts, $80.6\%$ have a defending majority.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-verdict-distribution} gives the full distribution.

\paragraph{Lower scores coexist with greater reply engagement.}
In high-agreement posts, majority comments have an equal-post mean score
of $39.55$, compared with $-10.25$ for minority comments
(Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). The ordering reverses for reply
engagement: minority comments average $0.73$ direct replies and a maximum
reply depth of $1.03$, compared with $0.15$ and $0.17$ for majority comments.
Low evaluation thus coexists with more replies and deeper discussions.

Under low agreement, majority--minority differences are smaller. Scores
average $35.89$ versus $27.60$, direct-reply counts $0.28$ versus $0.32$,
and maximum reply depths $0.36$ versus $0.41$. The mean score gap is
$49.80$ points under high agreement and $8.29$ under low agreement, while
the differences in reply count and depth are also larger under high agreement.
These descriptive comparisons establish how reception varies with the
agreement context; they do not estimate an effect of agreement strength.
Posts without minority comments do not contribute a minority value
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:analytical-samples}). Supplementary analyses
show that early minority comments receive more replies than later ones,
so arrival timing and exposure remain relevant to these aggregate outcomes
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aita-arrival}).

\paragraph{More replies do not imply greater support.}
\label{subsec:res-rq2}
Among annotated replies in high-agreement AITA posts, challenges account
for approximately $21.8\%$ of replies to majority judgments, compared
with $73.7$--$77.3\%$ across the two minority directions
(Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-responses}(b)). Support, by contrast, is the
most common response to majority judgments. This pattern concerns responses
from other commenters: OP-authored pairs are excluded. OP participation
is common ($81.0\%$ of eligible AITA posts), reinforcing the importance of
this distinction (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-op-participation}).

Response proportions describe sampled eligible replies, not the probability
that a comment receives a challenge. The annotation frame is broader than
the $200$-verdict reception cohort and covers high-agreement posts only;
these results therefore do not establish a high--low difference in challenge
rates. We examine the two minority directions separately below.

Together, these results address \textbf{RQ1}: minority judgments receive

high-agreement reply sample, minority judgments predominantly receive
challenges rather than support.

\subsection{Blaming Versus Defending Dissent}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

\paragraph{Blaming dissent receives more replies and deeper reply trees.}
Both directions receive negative mean scores: $-10.65$ for blaming dissent
in posts with a defending majority and $-8.62$ for defending dissent in
posts with a blaming majority (Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}). The
pooled majority reference is $39.55$. Blaming and defending partition the
high-agreement minority, rather than representing additional agreement levels.

The more pronounced directional contrast is in engagement. Blaming dissent
averages $0.80$ direct replies versus $0.44$ for defending dissent, a
$1.83$-fold difference calculated from unrounded post-equal means.
Maximum reply depths average $1.12$ versus $0.70$, a $1.60$-fold difference.
The corresponding majority baselines are similar across the two post
contexts: $0.15$ and $0.16$ replies, with depths of $0.17$ in both.
These comparisons involve different story cohorts and potentially different
contributing post sets, rather than judgments randomly assigned to the
same story. They do not establish why blaming dissent receives more engagement.

\paragraph{Challenges predominate in replies to both minority directions.}
Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-responses}(b) shows a shared contrast with majority
judgments. Approximately $68.7\%$ of majority-parent replies support the
parent, while $21.8\%$ challenge it. For blaming minority parents,
$16.1\%$ support and $77.3\%$ challenge; for defending minority parents,
$18.6\%$ support and $73.7\%$ challenge. Thus, assigning blame against the
majority and resisting the majority's blame both predominantly elicit
challenging responses in the annotated sample.

The difference between the minority directions is smaller than their
contrast with the majority: blaming has a $3.6$-percentage-point higher
challenge proportion and a $2.5$-percentage-point lower support proportion
than defending, based on the rounded values. These are descriptive
differences; they do not by themselves establish a reliable directional
difference in response composition. The main pattern is the predominance
of support for majority judgments and of challenge for both minority directions.

Each minority direction contributes $2{,}000$ labeled pairs, while the
majority combines two equally sized strata ($4{,}000$ pairs). Proportions
weight pairs, retain all four response categories in the denominator,
and describe the first annotation sample. Information-seeking and other
responses constitute the remaining shares. Because this sample differs
from the reception cohort, the response proportions cannot identify the
composition of the additional replies associated with blaming dissent
in the full cohort.

\paragraph{Both minority directions end with higher expressed certainty.}
\label{subsec:res-certainty-contrasts}
Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-responses}(a) shows mean certainty across ten
approximately equal-count stages of each post's first-day verdict-comment
sequence. Majority certainty remains comparatively high and stable.
Both blaming and defending minority comments have higher mean certainty
at the final stage than at the initial stage, with fluctuations between
stages. Neither minority profile increases monotonically.

Blaming has a higher mean than defending at each plotted stage, but this
ordering does not establish different rates of increase. The profiles
use separate fixed cohorts of $659$ majority, $143$ blaming, and $55$
defending posts; their pointwise intervals do not test differences between
the groups or their changes. Both directions therefore show an
initial-to-final increase in mean expressed certainty, without establishing
distinct trajectories. These are successive comments from potentially
different participants, not evidence of increased certainty within individuals.

Full-period certainty distributions and annotation-version sensitivity
checks appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty-comparison};
all-agreement trend estimates appear in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty-progress}. The full-period directional
contrast is sensitive to annotation version and should not be interpreted
as evidence of different first-day trajectories.

more replies and deeper reply trees than defending dissent, while both
minority directions predominantly receive challenges. Both show higher
mean expressed certainty at the end of the first-day sequence, without
establishing different rates of increase between them.

\subsection{Cross-Community Generalizability}
\label{subsec:res-community}

\paragraph{The agreement-level pattern recurs.}
AITAH also combines lower minority scores with greater engagement under
high agreement (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview}).
Majority and minority equal-post mean scores are $16.14$ and $-1.89$,
respectively; direct-reply counts are $0.14$ and $0.48$; and maximum reply
depths are $0.15$ and $0.79$. Under low agreement, the reply-count difference
remains small ($0.233$ versus $0.237$), with depths of $0.37$ and $0.39$.
As in AITA, the high-agreement minority therefore occupies a distinctive
combination of lower evaluation and greater conversational engagement.

\paragraph{Directional ordering is shared, not its magnitude.}
Blaming dissent in AITAH receives a lower mean score than defending dissent
($-2.02$ versus $-0.25$), more direct replies ($0.50$ versus $0.28$), and
greater maximum reply depth ($0.81$ versus $0.51$)
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview}).
The blaming-to-defending reply ratio is $1.77$, compared with $1.83$ in
AITA. Both minority directions have negative equal-post mean scores in
both communities. These results show a shared
directional ordering alongside differences in recorded magnitudes.

Raw cross-community differences do not, by themselves, establish greater
tolerance, stronger normative pressure, or an effect of AITA's verdict
rule. The comparisons retain their analysis-specific observation frames
and may differ in audience, story composition, and exposure.

\paragraph{Certainty profiles do not transfer uniformly.}
Pooling agreement levels, the post fixed-effects analysis also evaluates
first-day certainty in AITAH. Blaming has a positive
fitted change of $0.036$ ($95\%$ post-bootstrap CI $[0.007,0.065]$;
Holm-adjusted $p=0.031$), whereas defending has an uncertain estimate of
$-0.015$ ($[-0.050,0.020]$; adjusted $p=0.413$). Thus, the combined
annotation sample does not support an increasing linear trend for every
community--direction group. The AITAH defending profile now includes
$155$ fixed-cohort posts across agreement levels; the separate full-period
high-agreement comparison still contains only $15$ defending posts
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high}). Significance in one group
and non-significance in another is not a test of a community difference
or community-by-direction interaction.

In this supplementary comparison, the recurring finding is the ordering of reception:
minority judgments attract lower scores and more reply engagement under
high agreement, and blaming dissent attracts more engagement than defending
dissent. The magnitudes vary between communities, while the certainty
profiles and exploratory slopes do not show the same pattern for every
community--direction group. The shared reception ordering does not
imply equivalent expression or reception environments, or identify their
causes. Supplementary cumulative-participation patterns appear in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cumulative-participation}.
