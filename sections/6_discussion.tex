\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our findings connect the reception of minority judgments, the moral
considerations introduced in replies, and readers' responses to displays
that include dissent. Together, these analyses suggest that social approval,
conversational engagement, and the range of reasons considered capture
different aspects of minority participation. We discuss what these
distinctions imply for interpreting and presenting online moral discussions,
while separating observed conversational patterns from readers'
self-reported experiences.

\subsection{Implications}
\label{subsec:discussion-findings}

Social approval and the exchange of moral reasons capture different aspects
of minority participation. Our reception analysis distinguishes the
agreement context from the moral direction of dissent. Majority--minority
gaps in scores, reply counts, and depth are larger in high-agreement
than low-agreement posts. Within high-agreement posts, blaming dissent
draws greater engagement than defending dissent, while
both predominantly receive challenges. These findings suggest that
comment scores alone provide an incomplete account of the exchanges
around dissent, and that the direction of a minority judgment matters
for how others respond. The contribution of this comparison is to
characterize variation across agreement contexts and moral directions,
extending the established distinction between approval and engagement.

The preliminary perspective annotations further suggest that replies
around minority judgments can broaden the considerations under discussion.
This contribution belongs to the exchange: an additional consideration
may be introduced by someone responding to a minority comment, rather
than by the minority commenter. The minority--majority difference in
addition rates narrows when support and challenge are examined separately.
This pattern suggests that the composition of replies is relevant to the
overall contrast, without establishing that challenge causes perspective
addition. Both supportive and challenging replies can extend a parent's
reasoning, so agreement with a judgment and contribution of an additional
consideration should be evaluated separately.

Reader responses likewise support examining reasons alongside agreement.
Readers shown a majority and a minority thread reported a broader range
of moral reasons than those shown two majority threads, consistent
with research linking minority influence to divergent thinking
\cite{nemeth1987divergent}. Among participants whose final judgments
agreed with the majority, mean certainty was lower in Balanced than in
Highly Scored. These are descriptive comparisons of post-exposure groups.
Reported consideration of alternative reasons can coexist with a final
judgment that agrees with the majority.

These findings motivate evaluating thread-selection approaches that expose
readers to different moral reasons alongside comment scores. Presenting
a majority and a minority comment with their replies is one candidate;
most participants in both survey conditions preferred one comment from
each side. Such evaluations should examine which considerations readers
encounter and use in their decisions, since opposing verdicts alone do not
guarantee distinct reasoning. They should also assess contributors'
experiences and willingness to continue participating: the prevalence of
challenging replies motivates this question but does not itself establish
a participation cost.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

Reception contrasts may reflect story composition, unobserved exposure,
and unequal follow-up. Majority/minority labels use final judgments, and
temporal profiles compare successive comments rather than within-person
change. Future research could track the judgments and ranking cues visible
when each comment is posted and measure reception over matched follow-up
windows. Such data would help distinguish final minority status from the
opinion environment encountered by contributors. Following participation
and withdrawal could also examine whether challenging exchanges discourage
further contributions, rather than assuming that challenge itself imposes
a participation cost.

LLM-derived measures need further independent validation.
Moral-consideration categories are post-specific, and additions relative
to a parent comment do not measure argument quality or novelty across the
whole discussion. Future work should validate annotations with independent
human coders and examine whether added considerations are relevant,
well-supported, and taken up in subsequent replies. Extending the analysis
beyond direct replies would help establish whether these considerations
remain isolated contributions or become part of the discussion's shared
reasoning.

The survey covers $80$ participants and four scenarios, varying comment
content, replies, and scores together. Responses are self-reported without
a baseline judgment, alignment groups are defined after exposure, and the
unadjusted subgroup comparisons are exploratory. Larger studies across
more varied scenarios and reader populations should use prespecified
outcomes and comparisons, and vary thread content and social cues
independently to identify their respective contributions. Open-ended
accounts of the reasons informing participants' decisions could complement
ratings of new considerations, while delayed measures could assess which
reasons participants retain. These extensions would clarify when presenting
minority threads broadens consideration and how those considerations enter
decisions, without requiring a change in judgment as the criterion of value.
