\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Implications}
\label{subsec:discussion-findings}

Minority dissent separates social approval from conversational contribution.
In high-agreement posts, minority judgments receive lower scores yet attract
more replies and deeper discussion; replies to them also more often add
moral considerations. Blaming dissent draws greater engagement than
defending dissent, while both predominantly receive challenges. The moral
direction of dissent thus matters alongside its minority status. Supportive
and challenging replies can both extend the reasons under discussion,
making scores an incomplete indicator of a comment's conversational
contribution.

Reader responses reinforce the value of examining reasons alongside
agreement. Readers shown a majority and a minority thread reported a
broader range of moral considerations than those shown two majority
threads, consistent with research linking minority influence to divergent
thinking \cite{nemeth1987divergent}. Among majority-aligned readers,
certainty was lower in the mixed condition; among minority-aligned readers,
it was higher. Broader consideration can therefore accompany a less
certain majority-aligned judgment.

These findings motivate evaluating displays that present a majority and a
minority comment together with their replies. Most participants in both
conditions preferred one comment from each side. Such evaluations should
assess the range of considerations readers take into account alongside
possible participation costs for contributors receiving predominantly
challenging replies.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

Reception contrasts may reflect story composition, unobserved exposure,
and unequal follow-up. Majority/minority labels use final judgments, and
temporal profiles compare successive comments rather than within-person
change. LLM-derived measures need further independent validation;
moral-consideration categories are post-specific, and additions relative
to a parent comment do not measure argument quality.

The survey covers $80$ participants and four scenarios, varying comment
content, replies, and scores together. Responses are self-reported without
a baseline judgment, and alignment groups are defined after exposure;
subgroup contrasts are descriptive. The unadjusted comparisons are
exploratory. Future studies should vary judgment cues and comment features
independently and examine more diverse discussions and reader populations.
