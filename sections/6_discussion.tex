\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Key Findings and Implications}
\label{subsec:discussion-findings}

\runinheading{Social approval and conversational contribution.}
Minority judgments receive less approval but attract more and deeper
discussion in high-agreement posts. Their replies also more often add
moral considerations beyond the parent comment. These patterns distinguish
approval of a judgment from the reasons developed in the surrounding
discussion. Scores alone therefore provide an incomplete picture of how
dissent contributes to moral discussion.

\runinheading{The moral direction of dissent.}
Blaming dissent attracts more replies and deeper discussion than defending
dissent, while challenges predominate in replies to both. Contesting an
exculpatory consensus may invite demands to justify blame
\cite{malle2022socially}. Higher expressed certainty among later minority
comments may also reflect selective participation: tentative dissenters
may be less willing to contribute in an unfavorable opinion climate or
when anticipating sanctions \cite{matthes2010spiral,neubaum2018sanctions}.
This remains a hypothesis because we do not observe who refrains from
commenting.

\runinheading{Community rules and the reception of dissent.}
AITA formalizes collective judgment through a bot that assigns a judgment
flair after 18 hours based on the highest-scoring comment
\cite{nguyen2022mapping}. Our exploratory comparisons of recorded reception
by comment arrival time showed no clear break around this nominal
threshold. Because reception measures aggregate subsequent activity, this
does not test an immediate effect of flair assignment or rule out a broader
role for the rule. The expectation of an official judgment may shape
participation and responses throughout a discussion. Such cues offer one
possible mechanism to investigate alongside the more negative reception
of minority judgments observed in AITA than in AITAH. However, our
comparison cannot separate their role from differences in membership,
moderation, content, and exposure. Future research should examine how
formal judgment cues shape the reception of dissent.

\runinheading{Range of moral considerations and judgment certainty.}
Compared with readers shown two majority threads, those shown a minority
thread reported a broader range of moral considerations in both judgment
groups. Majority-aligned readers reported lower certainty, while
minority-aligned readers reported higher certainty. Broader consideration
can thus accompany a less certain majority judgment. This pattern aligns
with research linking minority influence to divergent thinking
\cite{nemeth1987divergent}; whether these self-reports translate into
better reasoning requires further study.

\runinheading{Designing exposure to competing reasons.}
Our survey findings motivate evaluating comment displays that present a
majority comment and a minority comment together, each accompanied by its
reply thread. Most participants in both conditions preferred one comment
from each side, indicating interest in such displays. Future evaluations
should assess the range of moral considerations readers take into account
alongside possible participation costs for contributors receiving
predominantly challenging replies.

\subsection{Moral Considerations in Cross-Posted Stories}
\label{subsec:discussion-cross-posted}

The same story can receive opposing dominant judgments across communities
while eliciting different moral considerations. Among 1,871 pairs have YA shares available in both communities, we examine an identical narrative about a firearm in a
bedside drawer during a family visit
(Appendix~\ref{subsec:res-duplicates}). The dominant judgment blames the
OP (YA $70\%$) in AITA but defends the OP (NA $60\%$) in AITAH.

The annotations make the considerations surrounding this contrast
concrete. Both post-specific taxonomies distinguish firearm storage,
parental supervision, and household autonomy, locating responsibility in
securing a dangerous item, supervising one's children, and controlling
access to one's home. The AITA taxonomy additionally separates shared
fault and foreseeable risk; the AITAH taxonomy separately identifies
limits to family obligations and the mother's favoritism. These
categories show how the moral dispute extends beyond choosing YA or NA
into the responsibilities and relationships used to justify a judgment.

In both communities, supportive and challenging replies add moral
considerations.
Among $31$ eligible AITA pairs, $6/10$ challenging replies and $5/15$
supportive replies add a moral consideration; among $96$ AITAH pairs, the
corresponding counts are $28/40$ and $11/41$. These orientation-specific
denominators retain unmapped pairs and exclude information-seeking and
other replies. Thus, each community's discussion can develop additional
reasons through agreement as well as disagreement.

The shared narrative elicited opposing dominant judgments alongside
different ways of framing responsibility. This observational case cannot
isolate a community effect, but it shows why a prevailing judgment provides
only a partial account of moral disagreement. Cross-posting makes
alternative framings of the same conflict visible, highlighting the value
of examining the reasons developed in comments and replies alongside the
final YA/NA balance.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

\runinheading{Scope of evidence and measurement.}
Our analyses characterize visible participation in two moral-judgment
communities and survey responses from $80$ participants across four
scenarios. Temporal analyses select posts with persistent group
representation; reply annotations cover eligible exchanges excluding OP
participation. The annotations capture expressed certainty, reply
orientation, and reasons added relative to a parent comment, rather than
private confidence, argument quality, or novelty across an entire
discussion. Temporal profiles describe successive comments rather than
within-person change, while the survey measures self-reported responses
without a baseline judgment. LLM classification errors and post-specific
categories may affect estimates. Human review used development-set
support/challenge pairs; independent validation and more diverse samples
would strengthen reliability assessment and generalizability.

\runinheading{Interpreting the observed differences.}
Blaming and defending dissent occur in different post sets, so their
reception contrasts may also reflect differences in stories, arguments,
and expression. Final observed judgments define majority/minority status,
not the distribution visible when a comment appeared. Individual exposure
is unobserved, and reception measures have unequal follow-up periods.
These distinctions limit attributing reception differences to dissent
direction or perceived minority status alone. The survey likewise varies
comment content, replies, and scores together; its alignment groups are
defined after exposure, so subgroup contrasts do not identify causal
effects. The unadjusted survey comparisons are exploratory. Future studies
could vary judgment cues and comment features independently to clarify
the mechanisms underlying these patterns.
