\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Blaming and Defending Dissent Over Time}
\label{subsec:discussion-temporal-dissent}
The temporal results show that minority voice did not always decline as the discussion progressed. In AITA, blaming dissent generally decreased, while defending dissent increased in the all-agreement and low-agreement discussions. In high-agreement discussions, defending dissent declined at first, but then increased and ended at a level close to where it started.

Agreement level provides further context for these trajectories. Changes in blaming and defending shares were larger in low-agreement AITA discussions, while high-agreement discussions showed smaller absolute changes. In the first-day certainty profiles, both types of AITA dissent have higher estimated means in the final stage than in the first, but the intervening changes are not monotonic. These profiles compare a fixed set of posts with certainty-annotated minority comments in every stage, so the endpoint differences are not due to posts entering or leaving the averages. They remain descriptive differences in expressed language, not evidence of increasing certainty within individuals, and need not generalize to discussions with intermittent or disappearing dissent.

The community comparison sets a boundary on these findings. Across all agreement levels combined, AITAH showed the opposite directional pattern: blaming share increased while defending share decreased. This suggests that the temporal development of moral dissent may differ across communities. Taken together, these observations suggest that minority participation should be understood in relation to both the direction of dissent and the community in which it occurs.

One possible explanation for the certainty trajectories is selective participation rather than increasing certainty within individuals. Our curves aggregate comments from different authors. Prior research links unfavorable opinion climates to reduced expression particularly among people with lower attitude certainty, and identifies anticipated sanctions as a potential deterrent to minority expression \cite{matthes2010spiral,neubaum2018sanctions}. Voluntary participation can therefore shape whose opinions become visible \cite{settle2025promise}. In AITA's more challenging reception environment, tentative dissenters may be less willing to contribute, leaving later contributions more assertively expressed. A less challenging environment could instead permit continued entry of more tentative minority voices. This possibility applies to both blaming and defending dissent, but the recalculated profiles do not establish that certainty increases more strongly in AITA than in AITAH.

This account remains a hypothesis. Higher challenge levels alone do not explain an increase across discussion stages; such a trajectory would additionally require changing participation or increasingly visible cues to the costs of dissent. We do not observe who considered commenting but remained silent, what earlier comments and replies commenters saw, or their private certainty. Challenges also need not be personal attacks. Consequently, the community contrast does not establish that anticipated sanctions selected participants, and differences in participants, topics, or expression strategies remain alternative explanations.

\subsection{Moral Considerations Beyond the Judgment}
\label{subsec:discussion-perspectives}

A judgment summarizes a position on the OP's conduct, but does not capture
all the considerations used to assess it. Our exploratory annotations of
cross-posted stories illustrate this distinction
(Appendix~\ref{subsec:res-duplicates}). In one story involving a firearm
in a bedside drawer, the annotated comments invoke firearm-storage
responsibility, parental supervision, and household autonomy. These
considerations concern different responsibilities and rights within the
same conflict, illustrating why a shared narrative can invite multiple
lines of moral reasoning.

This example helps clarify the purpose of measuring additional perspectives:
a reply can add a consideration without necessarily reversing the judgment
or challenging the comment. Whether it does so requires examining the
comment--reply pair, rather than inferring novelty from the distribution
of themes across a discussion. The cross-posted example is illustrative;
its independently learned taxonomies and lack of comment-alignment
breakdowns do not establish that minority comments uniquely supply these
considerations or that the communities systematically differ. The survey
addresses the complementary question of whether participants encounter
new perspectives in the displayed reply threads and how much those perspectives
inform their decisions.
