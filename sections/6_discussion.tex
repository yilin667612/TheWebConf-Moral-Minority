\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Key Findings and Implications}
\label{subsec:discussion-findings}

\runinheading{Social approval and conversational contribution.}
Minority judgments receive less approval but generate more and deeper
discussion in high-agreement posts. Their replies also more often add
perspectives beyond those expressed in the parent comment. Together,
these findings distinguish approval, interaction, and perspective addition
as separate dimensions of a comment's contribution. Challenges dominate
replies to minority judgments, and the perspective-addition gap narrows
within reply orientations. The conversational contribution of dissent
therefore includes the reasons it elicits from others. Evaluating comments
through scores alone can overlook this contribution.

\runinheading{The moral direction of dissent.}
Blaming dissent attracts more replies and deeper discussion than defending
dissent, while challenges predominate in replies to both. The direction
of disagreement thus matters alongside minority status. Blaming dissent
assigns responsibility to an OP whom most commenters defend, contesting
an exculpatory consensus. Its greater engagement is consistent with
accounts of public blame as a socially accountable act that invites
demands for justification \cite{malle2022socially}.

Selective participation offers one possible explanation for the higher
expressed certainty of later minority comments. Prior work links
unfavorable opinion climates to reduced expression among people with
lower attitude certainty and identifies anticipated sanctions as a
deterrent to minority expression
\cite{matthes2010spiral,neubaum2018sanctions}. Tentative dissenters may
refrain from commenting, leaving more assertive contributions visible.
This remains a hypothesis: our profiles aggregate successive comments, and
we observe neither silent readers nor the cues they encountered.

\runinheading{Perspective broadening and judgment certainty.}
Readers shown a minority judgment reported broader perspective
consideration in both reported judgment groups. Among those who agreed
with the majority, this broader consideration accompanied lower
certainty; minority-aligned readers instead reported higher certainty.
This distinction makes certainty informative alongside judgment direction:
readers can report considering more reasons while ultimately endorsing
the majority position. The broadening pattern is consistent with research
linking minority influence to divergent thinking
\cite{nemeth1987divergent}. Our survey captures reported consideration
and confidence after exposure; whether these translate into better
reasoning requires further study.

\runinheading{Designing exposure to competing reasons.}
These findings motivate interfaces that make competing reasons accessible
alongside highly approved judgments. Most survey participants in both
conditions preferred one comment from each side, supporting interest in
such exposure. A useful design direction is to surface a dissenting
comment with its reply context, where both challenges and support can
introduce further considerations. The observational and survey findings
address complementary parts of this process rather than an established
causal pathway. Future evaluations should assess perspective broadening
alongside possible participation costs for contributors who receive
predominantly challenging replies.

\subsection{Moral Considerations in Cross-Posted Stories}
\label{subsec:discussion-cross-posted}

The same story can receive opposing dominant judgments across communities
while eliciting different moral considerations. Among four exploratory
matched stories, we examine an identical narrative about a firearm in a
bedside drawer during a family visit
(Appendix~\ref{subsec:res-duplicates}). Its recorded YA share is $70\%$ in
AITA and $40\%$ in AITAH: the dominant judgment blames the OP in AITA
but defends the OP in AITAH.

The annotations make the considerations surrounding this contrast
concrete. Both post-specific taxonomies distinguish firearm storage,
parental supervision, and household autonomy, locating responsibility in
securing a dangerous item, supervising one's children, and controlling
access to one's home. The AITA taxonomy additionally separates shared
fault and foreseeable risk; the AITAH taxonomy separately identifies
limits to family obligations and the mother's favoritism. These
categories show how the moral dispute extends beyond choosing YA or NA
into the responsibilities and relationships used to justify a judgment.

Both communities also show perspective addition in supportive and
challenging replies.
Among $31$ eligible AITA pairs, $6/10$ challenging replies and $5/15$
supportive replies add a perspective; among $96$ AITAH pairs, the
corresponding counts are $28/40$ and $11/41$. These orientation-specific
denominators retain unmapped pairs and exclude information-seeking and
other replies. Thus, each community's discussion can develop additional
reasons through agreement as well as disagreement.

Together, the contrasting judgments and annotated considerations suggest
that community context may matter for which moral reasons enter a
discussion and how the OP's conduct is evaluated. This is an illustrative
connection: audiences, posting times, exposure, and other factors also
differ, and neither post meets our high-agreement threshold. Independently
learned taxonomies and aggregate addition counts do not establish which
specific considerations were newly added or whether they account for the
judgment difference.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

\runinheading{Observational data and selection.}
Our archives capture visible participation in two moral-judgment
communities. Final observed judgments define alignment, while scores and
reply trees have unequal follow-up periods. Topic, author, timing, and
exposure differences can contribute to the reception contrasts. Fixed
post cohorts improve temporal comparability but select discussions with
persistent group representation. Reply annotations further describe
eligible exchanges excluding OP participation, rather than every response
or the probability of receiving a reply.

\runinheading{Annotation and measurement.}
LLM annotations operationalize expressed certainty, reply orientation,
and perspective addition. Development-set review does not provide
independent held-out validation. Post-specific taxonomies measure
considerations absent from a parent comment, not novelty across a whole
discussion or the quality of an argument. Unmapped texts and annotation
version differences can affect estimates. Linguistic certainty also
differs from private confidence; successive comments cannot establish
within-person change.

\runinheading{Survey scope.}
The survey includes $80$ participants and four scenarios, with a
manipulation combining comment content, replies, and observed scores.
It measures self-reported consideration and certainty after exposure,
without a baseline judgment or a test of reasoning quality. Alignment
groups are defined after exposure, so their contrasts do not identify
causal subgroup effects. The $16$ unadjusted comparisons are exploratory.
Larger studies across more scenarios, with baseline measures and
independent manipulation of thread features, can test the robustness and
mechanisms of these patterns.
