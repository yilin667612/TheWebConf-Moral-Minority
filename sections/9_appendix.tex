\section{Early Verdict Leaders and Final Alignment}
\label{sec:appendix-stabilization}

Early verdict leaders are more predictive of the final winner in
high-agreement than low-agreement posts
(Figure~\ref{fig:consensus-stabilization}). In the reported high-agreement
series, the first comment agrees with the final winner in $86.5\%$ of posts,
rising to $95.8\%$ after three comments. In low-agreement posts, the
correspondence is $52.1\%$ after one comment and $83.5\%$ after $201$.
This describes the relationship between arrival-ordered judgments and final
alignment; it does not establish what comments a user saw or how they perceived
the opinion climate.

For this analysis, retained top-level NA/YA verdict comments are ordered by
arrival, with INFO excluded. At each checkpoint, the running YA share
determines the leading side, which is compared with the final dominant
verdict. Each post contributes one observation per checkpoint, so larger
threads do not receive greater weight. Odd checkpoints
($1,3,5,11,\ldots,201$) avoid running ties and parity-driven oscillation.
Cohorts are defined by the final share recomputed from these comments,
with at least $200$ retained verdicts per post. Complete correspondence
at a checkpoint is $100\%$; the $50\%$ line is a coin-flip reference,
not an estimate of how informed participants are. A match at one checkpoint
does not guarantee that the current leader cannot subsequently change.

\section{Verdict Tags in AITA and AITAH}
\label{sec:appendix-tags}

Both communities ask commenters to open a top-level reply with one of seven
verdict tags (Table~\ref{tab:tags}). Six of them assign blame and enter the
analysis; \emph{INFO} alone declines to judge and is discarded. \emph{YWBTA}
and \emph{YWNBTA} are the hypothetical forms, used when the OP asks about an
action not yet taken rather than one already taken; because they point the
blame in the same direction as \emph{NTA} and \emph{YTA}, they join those tags
in the exculpating group $\mathrm{NA}$ and the inculpating group $\mathrm{YA}$
respectively (\S\ref{subsec:dataset}).

The vocabularies are otherwise identical, with one difference in surface form:
AITAH additionally accepts H-suffixed aliases of the four OP-directed tags
(\emph{NTAH}, \emph{YTAH}, \emph{YWNBTAH}, \emph{YWBTAH}), which we normalise to
their unsuffixed counterparts before any analysis. \emph{NAH}, \emph{ESH}, and
\emph{INFO} have no suffixed variant in either community.

\section{Post-Content Comparability}
\label{sec:appendix-content}

The two communities discuss substantially, but not identically, the same
situations. Figure~\ref{fig:umap} projects $768$-dimensional
\texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5} post-text embeddings into two dimensions with UMAP,
coloring points by source community.
A classifier recovering a post's source community from its
embedding alone reaches a ROC-AUC of $0.613$ under BGE and $0.568$ under E5,
against a chance level of $0.50$, and the UMAP projection in
Figure~\ref{fig:umap} shows why: the two communities interleave throughout the
map, including within the small peripheral clusters that correspond to
recurring situation types.

The difference that remains is nonetheless systematic rather than noise.
Maximum mean discrepancy and energy-distance tests reject equality of the two
embedding distributions under both encoders (permutation $p=.005$ in every
case). Topic modelling locates the difference in composition rather than in
kind: the communities share most topics, but AITAH leans towards romantic and
intimate relationships, while AITA carries relatively more everyday disputes
over housing, money, food, transport, and family obligation (Jensen--Shannon
distance between the topic distributions $0.187$ under BGE, $0.206$ under E5).

We therefore do not treat the two communities as topically interchangeable.
The community extension compares reception and expression using the same
operational definitions (\S\ref{subsec:method-community}). Cross-posted stories
are reserved for illustrative cases (\S\ref{subsec:res-duplicates}), rather
than a quantitative test that removes differences between communities.

\section{Certainty Annotation}
\label{sec:appendix-annotation}

Certainty scores for the annotated subset (\S\ref{subsec:dataset}) are produced
with the prompt reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt}. The prompt first
restates the verdict vocabulary, then defines the four-point scale in terms of
how the commenter hedges rather than what they conclude, so that a confident
\emph{YTA} and a confident \emph{NTA} score alike. Level $4$ marks a single
judgment asserted in a confident tone, level $3$ a single judgment with
qualifications, level $2$ a leaning that still admits an alternative, and level
$1$ an unresolved or self-contradicting judgment. Worked examples are supplied
at each level, and the annotation is returned as JSON with a free-text
rationale alongside the score.
The displayed examples are abridged. The placeholders \{post\} and
\{comment\} are filled with the submission text and the top-level verdict
comment being scored, respectively.

\section{Comparison of Expressed Certainty}
\label{sec:appendix-certainty-comparison}

This analysis compares the two directions of dissent over the full observed
period, separately from the supplementary cumulative-participation analysis
in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cumulative-participation}.
The $1{,}195$-post low/high annotation dataset contributes $1{,}169$ posts
and $104{,}135$ valid minority certainty observations after the exclusions
in Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}. Within-post proportions at each certainty
level are averaged with equal post weight
(Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high}). Mean certainty is retained as a
complementary summary rather than the primary interpretation of the ordinal
scale.

\paragraph{Estimation and uncertainty.}
For each community and agreement band, we calculate blaming minus defending
differences in mean certainty and in the certainty-$4$ proportion. Percentile
$95\%$ confidence intervals use $10{,}000$ bootstrap replicates, independently
resampling whole posts within each directional cohort. The same post weights
are used across measures in a replicate; contrast intervals are calculated
from replicate-level differences. These are marginal descriptive intervals,
without joint multiplicity adjustment or population reweighting.
Whether separate intervals exclude zero is not a test of a
direction-by-agreement or direction-by-community interaction.

Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high} uses the low/high agreement definitions
in Section~\ref{subsec:method}, excluding intermediate bands and exact ties.
Panel A colors encode certainty levels rather than dissent direction;
segments of at least $7\%$ are labeled. Panel B reports differences in
percentage points. Displayed counts list contributing posts in
blaming/defending order. The dagger
and hollow marker identify the $15$-post AITAH high-agreement defending
cohort. The figure describes full-period between-cohort contrasts, not
temporal trajectories.

\paragraph{AITA.}
In high-agreement posts, the certainty-$4$ proportions are $66.1\%$ for
blaming and $58.1\%$ for defending dissent, a difference of $8.0$ percentage
points ($95\%$ CI $[3.9,12.2]$), based on $506$ and $135$ posts.
Under low agreement, the values are $71.7\%$ and $70.5\%$; the difference
of $1.2$ percentage points ($[-1.6,4.1]$) remains uncertain, based on
$134$ and $137$ posts. Mean certainty is $3.564$ versus $3.488$ under high
agreement ($\Delta=0.075$, CI $[0.021,0.131]$), and $3.669$ versus $3.650$
under low agreement ($\Delta=0.018$, CI $[-0.012,0.050]$).

\paragraph{AITAH.}
The certainty-$4$ proportion difference is $12.0$ percentage points
($95\%$ CI $[4.8,20.0]$) under high agreement and $4.0$ percentage points
($[-1.2,9.0]$) under low agreement. Mean certainty is $3.626$ versus $3.505$
under high agreement ($\Delta=0.121$, CI $[0.022,0.228]$), and $3.673$ versus
$3.609$ under low agreement ($\Delta=0.064$, CI $[-0.001,0.128]$).
These comparisons use $184/15$ and $28/30$ blaming/defending posts,
respectively. The high-agreement defending cohort is particularly small:
an interval excluding zero does not remove the limitation of only $15$ posts.

\paragraph{Annotation-version sensitivity.}
The dataset combines \texttt{v4} and \texttt{v4.1} annotations. Restricting
high-agreement AITA to \texttt{v4} yields a certainty-$4$ difference of
$5.0$ percentage points ($95\%$ CI $[-0.7,10.5]$) and a mean-certainty
difference of $0.054$ points ($[-0.018,0.123]$), based on $315/71$ posts.
The positive point estimates remain, but their intervals include zero.
Different posts received the two annotation versions, so version and post
composition cannot be separated. These full-period contrasts do not
establish increasing certainty, temporal convergence, or individual
attitude change.

To separate window sensitivity from changes in contributing posts, we also
hold the post set fixed to first-18-hour contributors, using the same post
weights for both windows in each bootstrap replicate. Within high-agreement
AITA, the full-period contrast is then $0.071$ points rather than $0.090$
in the first 18 hours, a change of $-0.019$ ($95\%$ paired-window bootstrap

Thus, including later observations attenuates these aggregate gaps even
when post composition is held fixed. This does not demonstrate convergence
at 18 hours: the full-period estimate includes the early observations, and

comment fractions used to describe coverage, not the equal-post weighting
used for certainty estimates. Observed comment times alone do not establish
equal or complete follow-up across posts.




\section{Direct-Response Annotation}
\label{sec:appendix-rq2-annotation}

This section documents the current prompt template for the direct-response
analysis in \S\ref{subsec:method-rq2}. It classifies a child reply's main
communicative response to its immediate parent as agreement/support,
disagreement/challenge, clarification/information-seeking, or other.
The original post provides context. The template supplies no explicit
minority/majority labels, agreement-level metadata, or comment scores;
these analytical variables are kept separate from the annotation input.

The three high-agreement annotation samples use prompt version
\texttt{v0.3.3}. This version distinguishes a reply's central stance from
local refinements: a qualification preserving the parent's central position
can remain support, whereas a substantive rebuttal of that position,
reasoning, or recommendation can be challenge.

\section{Comment Retrieval Timing}
\label{sec:appendix-Retrieval Timing}

Each comment in our dataset contains two timestamps: \texttt{created\_utc}, indicating when the comment was posted, and \texttt{retrieved\_on}, indicating when it was retrieved by the collection system. We define retrieval delay as the difference between these timestamps. This distinction matters because comment scores and reply activity can continue to accumulate after a comment is posted. Recorded reception measures may therefore reflect different observation ages.
Our timestamp analysis identifies two collection regimes, separated by a transition in June 2023. Through May 2023, comments were generally retrieved retrospectively, with delays of tens or hundreds of days. From July 2023 onward, collection was nearly immediate: virtually all comments were retrieved within one hour, with monthly mean delays generally on the order of tens of seconds. Both AITA and AITAH exhibit this transition.
The baseline score, direct-reply, and reply-depth analyses pool comments
with equal comment weight; they do not average post-level means. This
aggregation does not remove differences in observation age. We therefore
treat recorded reception measures as observations from the available archive
rather than standardized lifetime outcomes, and interpret differences in
absolute scores and reply counts with caution.
