\section{Certainty Contrasts and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:appendix-certainty-comparison}

In high-agreement AITA posts, the mean within-post proportion of minority
comments at certainty level $4$ is $66.1\%$ for blaming and $58.1\%$ for
defending: a difference of $8.0$ percentage points ($95\%$ whole-post
bootstrap CI $[3.9,12.2]$). Under low agreement, the contrast is smaller
and uncertain ($1.2$ percentage points; CI $[-1.6,4.1]$). These estimates
weight posts equally and use the full observed annotation period; they
do not measure differences in first-day rates of change.

Directional contrasts use all observed minority comments with valid labels,
without an arrival-time cutoff. We average within-post certainty
distributions equally across posts, excluding posts without valid minority
observations, and compare the proportion at level $4$ and mean certainty.
Within each community and agreement band, the $95\%$ percentile intervals
for blaming-minus-defending differences independently resample whole posts
within each direction, preserving within-post dependence. The differing
sampling fractions are not used to estimate community-wide certainty.

Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high} reports post-equal full-period
distributions. Blaming-minus-defending contrasts use $10{,}000$ independent
whole-post resamples within directional cohorts (seed $20260922$), with
replicate-level differences and marginal percentile intervals. These are
not interaction tests. The small high-agreement AITAH defending cohort
is flagged even when its contrast interval excludes zero.

Restricting high-agreement AITA to \texttt{v4} gives a certainty-$4$
difference of $5.0$ percentage points (CI $[-0.7,10.5]$) and a mean difference
of $0.054$ ($[-0.018,0.123]$). Version and post composition are confounded.
Sensitivity analyses compare full-period estimates with arrivals
in $[0,18)$ hours, using fixed final-verdict labels. The same cutoff in AITAH
defines a comparison window, not a verdict-disclosure event.


Holding the post set fixed to first-18h contributors, adding later comments
reduces the high-agreement mean-certainty gap by $0.019$ points in AITA
(paired-window CI $[-0.035,-0.005]$) and $0.034$ in AITAH
($[-0.061,-0.010]$). This is not a test of convergence at 18 hours or a
causal effect of verdict disclosure. Pooled minority-comment fractions
arriving after 18h are $10.3\%$/$13.4\%$ in AITA/AITAH; after 24h they are
$4.3\%$/$7.0\%$.

\section{Certainty Profiles and Trends}
\label{sec:appendix-certainty-progress}

Across all agreement levels in the broader AITA annotation archive, both
fixed directional cohorts show positive post fixed-effects trends. The
fitted first-to-last-stage change is $0.039$ for blaming ($95\%$
post-bootstrap CI $[0.021,0.056]$) and $0.059$ for defending
($[0.038,0.080]$). Both survive Holm correction across the four
community--direction slopes. The profiles are not monotonic, and the larger
defending point estimate does not itself establish a directional difference
in trends. These pooled-agreement estimates do not replace the high-agreement
profiles or separate high/low full-period contrasts.

\paragraph{Certainty profiles do not transfer uniformly.}
Pooling agreement levels, the post fixed-effects analysis also evaluates
first-day certainty in AITAH. Blaming has a positive
fitted change of $0.036$ ($95\%$ post-bootstrap CI $[0.007,0.065]$;
Holm-adjusted $p=0.031$), whereas defending has an uncertain estimate of
$-0.015$ ($[-0.050,0.020]$; adjusted $p=0.413$). Thus, the combined
annotation sample does not support an increasing linear trend for every
community--direction group. The AITAH defending profile now includes
$155$ fixed-cohort posts across agreement levels; the separate full-period
high-agreement comparison still contains only $15$ defending posts
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high}). Significance in one group
and non-significance in another is not a test of a community difference
or community-by-direction interaction.

The broader all-agreement analysis includes intermediate-agreement
annotated posts without population reweighting. As for the high-agreement
profile, each community--direction curve retains a fixed set of posts with
scored comments in every first-day stage. Stage means receive equal post
weight, and a stage without scored minority comments is undefined, not zero.

Profiles use the fixed-stage eligibility rule in
Section~\ref{subsec:method-annotation}. A comment at zero-based rank $r$
among $m$ retained first-day verdicts is assigned to stage
$\lfloor10r/m\rfloor$. Resampling retains each post's complete sequence.
Pointwise intervals use $2{,}000$ post-bootstrap
replicates for all agreement levels (seed $20261007$) and $4{,}000$ for the
high-agreement AITA profile in Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-responses}A
(seed $20261008$). The latter includes separate
majority/blaming/defending cohorts, not a common matched set. Its display
range is $3.2$--$3.9$ on the original $1$--$4$ scale.

Within each all-agreement community--direction cohort, we fit
$\overline C_{p,s}=\alpha_p+\beta(s/9)+\epsilon_{p,s}$ for $s=0,\ldots,9$,
weighting post-stages equally. Thus $\beta$ is the fitted first-to-last-stage
change, not an hourly slope. Intervals use $20{,}000$ whole-post percentile
resamples; two-sided tests use post-cluster standard errors and $G-1$
degrees of freedom. Holm correction covers four slopes; displayed intervals
are unadjusted. Separate slopes do not test between-group differences or
imply monotonic or within-person change. Fixed cohorts favor discussions
with persistent scored expression; available-post profiles have different,
potentially changing compositions.

\section{Verdict Distributions}
\label{sec:appendix-verdict-distribution}

In the AITA cohort, $80.6\%$ of high-agreement posts are NA-dominant.
Post frequency and discussion volume are distinct: NA-dominant posts
are more numerous, whereas the average retained verdict count per post
is higher toward the YA end, particularly near unanimous YA judgments.
This distribution does not establish a community-wide aversion to blame.
The stories are supplied by the people whose conduct is being judged.
Research on authors' dual roles as characters and narrators
\cite{giorgi2023author}, and on selective retelling and possible
validation-seeking \cite{haworth2021reasonability}, provides context for
the imbalance without identifying the motives of individual posters.

Figure~\ref{fig:verdict-distributions} uses one-percentage-point
bins of stored final YA share, including exact ties and the endpoint $1$.
Bars count posts; the line divides stored verdict totals by posts per bin.
Both communities concentrate near the NA end, while verdict volume per post
is higher toward the YA end.

\section{Reception in AITAH}
\label{sec:appendix-aitah-reception}
\label{subsec:res-community}

The following figures apply the AITA definitions and post-equal aggregation
to AITAH. Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview} combines the agreement
and directional comparisons, using a common high-agreement majority
baseline across NA- and YA-dominant posts. Blaming and defending partition
the high-agreement minority; absent minority cells are excluded, not zero.
Boxes show interquartile ranges of post-level means, with median lines
and whiskers extending to observed values within $1.5$ interquartile ranges.
Outlier points are hidden but retained in the means. Axis ranges are
adjusted to AITAH and differ from Figure~\ref{fig:reception-overview}.

\paragraph{The agreement-level pattern recurs.}
AITAH also combines lower minority scores with greater engagement under
high agreement (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview}).
Majority and minority equal-post mean scores are $16.14$ and $-1.89$,
respectively; direct-reply counts are $0.14$ and $0.48$; and maximum reply
depths are $0.15$ and $0.79$. Under low agreement, the reply-count difference
remains small ($0.233$ versus $0.237$), with depths of $0.37$ and $0.39$.
As in AITA, the high-agreement minority therefore occupies a distinctive
combination of lower evaluation and greater conversational engagement.

\paragraph{Directional ordering is shared, not its magnitude.}
Blaming dissent in AITAH receives a lower mean score than defending dissent
($-2.02$ versus $-0.25$), more direct replies ($0.50$ versus $0.28$), and
greater maximum reply depth ($0.81$ versus $0.51$)
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:aitah-reception-overview}).
The blaming-to-defending reply ratio is $1.77$, compared with $1.83$ in
AITA. Both minority directions have negative equal-post mean scores in
both communities. These results show a shared
directional ordering alongside differences in recorded magnitudes.

Raw cross-community differences do not, by themselves, establish greater
tolerance, stronger normative pressure, or an effect of AITA's verdict
rule. The comparisons retain their analysis-specific observation frames
and may differ in audience, story composition, and exposure.

\paragraph{Comment timing.}
\label{sec:appendix-timing}
In the $200$-verdict cohort, median times to $50\%$/$90\%$ of observed
top-level comments are $7.3$/$17.7$ hours in AITA and $6.7$/$20.1$ in AITAH.
Median last-comment times are $52.5$ versus $158.0$ days, but the difference

\paragraph{Retrieval timing.}
\label{sec:appendix-retrieval-timing}
Retrieval delay is \texttt{retrieved\_on} minus \texttt{created\_utc}.
Both archives shift around June 2023 from retrospective retrieval, often
after tens or hundreds of days, to near-immediate retrieval from July.
Post-equal weighting does not standardize observation age; absolute reception
values should not be interpreted as common-age or lifetime outcomes.

\section{Supplementary Community Comparison Methods}
\label{sec:appendix-community-comparison}

\paragraph{Content and audience comparisons.}
We apply the same definitions and measures in AITAH to assess which AITA
patterns recur. Supporting content and audience comparisons use the aligned
period 17 March 2021--23 December 2025; reception analyses retain their
specified frames. These supporting analyses compare post-text embeddings
and shared-user sets, rather than assuming equivalent content or audiences;
Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:supp-community} provides the diagnostics.

\paragraph{Matching cross-posted stories.}
Cross-posted stories are identified by identical title-and-body text after
Unicode NFKC normalization, case folding, and whitespace normalization,
excluding empty, deleted, or removed bodies. We require observed verdict
shares in both communities, without imputation; contextual case selection
additionally requires at least $20$ retained verdicts per submission.
Shared text permits comparison around the same narrative but does not
control audiences, posting times, or exposure. Community differences are
therefore observational, not estimates of rule effects. Public narratives
may remain identifiable after usernames are removed.

\paragraph{Illustrative cross-posted stories.}
\label{subsec:res-duplicates}


The exploratory perspective export contains four paired stories, each
posted in AITA and AITAH. Each post has an independently learned taxonomy,
derived from up to $40$ top-level comments, and multi-label annotations
of eligible top-level parent--direct reply pairs. These are model-generated
labels without independent human validation. Category names and category
frequencies are therefore not treated as directly comparable across
communities. Parent and child distributions describe separate sets of
unique comments, not changes within individuals. The export does not
break down these results by parent majority/minority alignment.

For the bedside-drawer story, the AITA export contains $31$ eligible pairs
and the AITAH export $96$. Both taxonomies include considerations concerning
firearm storage, parental supervision, and household autonomy. Within AITA,
$6/10$ challenging replies and $5/15$ supportive replies add a category
absent from their parent; the corresponding AITAH counts are $28/40$ and
$11/41$. These denominators include unmapped pairs, for which at least one
comment has no assigned category, and exclude information-seeking and
other responses. Thus both response orientations include added-perspective
pairs in this exploratory example. The exported summaries do not identify
which specific considerations were added in each pair; establishing those
connections requires checking the underlying comments.

The story is used to illustrate the range of annotated moral considerations,
not selected as representative of community differences. Its recorded YA
shares are $70\%$ in AITA and $40\%$ in AITAH, so neither submission is a
high-agreement case under our thresholds. This example therefore does not
extend the high-agreement reception findings to a matched-story comparison.

\section{Cumulative Minority Participation}
\label{sec:appendix-cumulative-participation}

High-agreement $200$-verdict posts contribute retained top-level judgments
with valid nonnegative elapsed times below $24$ hours. At each hourly
checkpoint, cumulative minority counts are divided by cumulative verdict
counts pooled across the cohort, using final alignment. Empty hours add
no counts. These are not post-equal or window-specific shares and have no
uncertainty bands; cumulative aggregation can obscure later variation.


Both AITA directions initially decline; blaming recovers modestly and
defending ends near its initial share. AITAH blaming rises modestly while
defending declines early. These trajectories do not establish withdrawal
by individuals, a regime change, or complete discussion follow-up.
