\label{sec:appendix-dimensions}

Table~\ref{tab:dimensions} summarizes the analytical dimensions and
measures; Table~\ref{tab:tags} lists the judgment tags used for matching.

\runinheading{Annotation samples.}
Certainty annotations cover sampled high- and low-agreement posts;
sample counts are reported in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:analytical-samples}.
Unless otherwise specified, all LLM-based annotations in this work use DeepSeek Flash\footnote{\url{https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing}} with temperature set to 0.

\runinheading{Annotation of reply stance.}
\label{subsec:appendix-reply-annotation}
Reply annotation uses three independently sampled sets of 16,000 pairs, balanced across community, dominant judgment, and comment
alignment in high-agreement posts.
Pairs involving the OP, the same author, or unverifiable identities are excluded.
Given the post as context, the LLM classifies each direct reply as support, challenge, information-seeking, or other relative to its parent comment.
Human verification of 100 reply pairs yielded 91\% agreement with model labels.
The prompt, execution settings, and verification procedure are detailed in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:supp-response-annotation}.

\runinheading{Annotation of moral reasons.}
\label{subsec:appendix-moral-annotation}
We label 7,023 eligible public reply pairs from 100 AITA posts, split equally between posts with more than 80\% NA and more than 80\% YA judgments.
As described in Section~\ref{subsec:method-annotation}, taxonomies are initialized from sampled top-level comments, iteratively revised using the remaining eligible top-level comments and their direct replies,
and frozen before final labeling.
A manual review of 50 randomly selected model classifications found 42 (84\%) reasonable and eight (16\%) partly reasonable or unreasonable.
Taxonomy construction, iterative revision, and validation scope are detailed in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:supp-perspective-annotation}.

\runinheading{Judgment matching.}
\label{sec:appendix-tags}
Short judgment codes are case-sensitive and word-bounded; full phrases
are case-insensitive. Hypothetical and H-suffixed variants are normalized
(Table~\ref{tab:tags}).

\runinheading{Arrival-time profiles.}
\label{sec:appendix-aita-arrival}
Comments posted in the first 24 hours are grouped into one-hour arrival
bins. Within each post and hour, means are computed separately for
high-agreement majority and minority comments and jointly for both sides
under low agreement. Nonempty post means receive equal weight; posts
can contribute to some hours but not others. Zero-reply comments remain
included. Scores and replies use all recorded activity, not a common
follow-up period or only replies received within the first day.

\runinheading{Inference.}
Reception contrasts use post/group means, CR1 post-cluster standard
errors, and two-sided Wald tests; Holm correction covers nine contrasts
per community. Certainty trends use post fixed effects, progress from
zero to one, and post-cluster tests with post-count minus one degrees
of freedom. Holm correction covers six slopes; the 20,000-replicate
post-bootstrap CIs are unadjusted. These are separate community and
slope estimates, not direct comparisons. Supplementary
Sections~\ref{sec:supp-certainty-analysis} and
\ref{sec:supp-reception-analysis} provide estimation details.

\runinheading{Certainty contrasts and sensitivity.}
\label{sec:appendix-certainty}
Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-low-high} compares full-period post-equal
certainty. The high-agreement AITA certainty-4 gap is 8.0 percentage
points (95\% CI [3.9,12.2]), but restricting annotations to version
\texttt{v4} yields 5.0 points ([-0.7,10.5]); version and post composition
are confounded. The AITAH high-agreement defending sample has 15 posts
for full-period contrasts and eight for stage analysis. No AITAH slope
survives Holm correction (Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-progress}). Detailed
sensitivity checks appear in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:supp-certainty-analysis}.

\runinheading{Reception in AITAH.}

\runinheading{Cross-posted stories: matching and selection.}
\label{subsec:res-duplicates}
\label{sec:appendix-community-comparison}
We match combined post titles and bodies across AITA and AITAH after
Unicode NFKC normalization, case folding, and whitespace normalization,
excluding empty, deleted, or removed bodies. Matches require observed
judgment shares in both communities, without imputation; contextual case
selection additionally requires at least $20$ retained judgments per post.
Shared narratives provide context for comparison, while audiences,
posting times, and exposure may differ. These comparisons are observational,
and public narratives may remain identifiable after usernames are removed.

\runinheading{Exploratory annotations of moral reasons.}
\label{subsec:appendix-cross-posted-annotations}
The export contains four paired stories ($8$ posts). Each post has an
independently learned taxonomy derived from up to $40$ comments, with
multi-label annotations of eligible comment--reply pairs. These
model-generated labels lack independent human validation. Category names
and frequencies are not treated as directly comparable across communities.
Comment and reply distributions describe separate sets of unique texts,
not changes within individuals; the export has no majority/minority
breakdown of parent comments.

A reply adds a moral reason when both texts map to at least one category
and the reply contains a category absent from its comment
(Section~\ref{subsec:method-annotation}). Support/challenge denominators
retain unmapped pairs, in which at least one text has no assigned category,
and exclude information-seeking and other replies.
Supplementary Section~\ref{subsec:supp-cross-posted-case} presents the
bedside-drawer case, its contrasting dominant judgments, annotated
considerations, and addition counts.
Aggregate summaries do not identify which consideration a particular
reply adds; that requires checking the underlying pair.

\section{Survey Experiment}
\label{app:survey}

\runinheading{Procedure and scenarios.}
Figure~\ref{fig:survey-procedure} summarizes the survey procedure.
Table~\ref{tab:survey-posts} documents the four scenarios (two YA
majorities and two NA majorities), featured-comment scores, and
participant allocation. Majority/minority labels and agreement
percentages were not shown to participants.

\runinheading{Participant demographics.}
Participants ranged in age from 19 to 58 years
($M=34.10$, $SD=9.27$), with 55.0\% aged 25--34. Thirty-eight
participants identified as White (47.5\%), 15 as Black (18.8\%),
15 as Asian (18.8\%), four as Mixed (5.0\%), and seven as another
ethnicity (8.8\%); one participant preferred not to disclose their
ethnicity (1.3\%). Participants resided across 23 countries, with
the largest groups residing in South Africa ($n=14$, 17.5\%) and
the United Kingdom ($n=12$, 15.0\%).

Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results-pooled} reports the survey outcomes using
