\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Minority Reception in Groups and Online Communities}
\label{subsec:related-minority-reception}
\label{subsec:related-online-engagement}

Social influence involves both reliance on others as evidence and normative pressures to gain acceptance \cite{deutsch1955normative,toelch2015conformity}. Individuals may align with a unanimous majority even on perceptual tasks \cite{asch1956independence}, yet consistent minorities can also influence majority responses \cite{moscovici1969minority}. Minority influence is more evident on private, indirect measures than on public responses \cite{wood1994minority}, distinguishing influence from public acceptance.

Group responses to dissent can combine attention with rejection. Schachter linked persistent dissent to efforts at persuasion and eventual rejection \cite{schachter1951deviation}. A replication found rejection and greater overall communication toward dissenters, but no significant role-by-time interaction \cite{wesselmann2014revisiting}; a uniform progression from engagement to exclusion cannot be assumed. Meta-analytic evidence also links smaller consistently dissenting factions to stronger rejection \cite{tata1996proportionate}.

Online feedback similarly separates evaluation from engagement. Prior ratings can influence later evaluations \cite{muchnik2013social,glenski2017rating}, and Reddit voting reflects local norms \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Responding, disliking, and flagging constitute distinct reactions \cite{kalch2017replying}; downvoted Reddit comments can receive more replies and larger reply trees \cite{davis2021emotional}. Conversation research further distinguishes broad participation from repeated exchanges \cite{backstrom2013threads,choi2015reddit}. Whereas thread-level controversy concerns disagreement or mixed feedback around an item \cite{hessel2019brewing}, our focus is how opposing judgments within a discussion are received. We identify numerical minorities through verdicts rather than scores, and distinguish reply volume from support or challenge. These measures characterize reception, not perceived opinion climates, conformity, or persuasion.

\subsection{Blame, Accountability, and the Expression of Dissent}
\label{subsec:related-selective-expression}

Blame is both a judgment of conduct and a socially regulated act. Malle and colleagues describe its dependence on norm violations, causality, intentionality, and reasons or preventability \cite{malle2014blame}. Public criticism can itself invite scrutiny: communities may demand justification and criticize unwarranted blame \cite{malle2022socially}. Blame also differentiates more finely among mental states than praise, although evidence for greater extremity is weaker \cite{guglielmo2019asymmetric}. Defending against blame is not equivalent to praise, but these accounts motivate examining blaming and defending dissent as distinct positions rather than interchangeable minorities. They do not predetermine which direction receives more acceptance. Research on moral rebels additionally distinguishes reactions from implicated participants and uninvolved observers \cite{monin2008rejection}, motivating attention to responder roles without equating that distinction with OP versus other commenters.

Anticipated evaluation can also shape expression. Accountability may encourage accommodation to a known audience, consideration of alternatives when audience views are unknown, or bolstering of prior commitments \cite{tetlock1989strategies}. Doubtful language is associated with fewer recommendations in online news discussions \cite{evans2023doubt}. These findings motivate studying expressed certainty, but neither blame nor accountability theory implies that one dissent direction must always sound more certain.

Participation may itself be selective. Matthes and colleagues associate opinion climate with expression among people with low or moderate, but not high, attitude certainty \cite{matthes2010spiral}. Anticipated personal attacks are linked to willingness to express minority opinions \cite{neubaum2018sanctions}, while voluntary participation may overrepresent strong views or low conflict avoidance \cite{settle2025promise}. These accounts suggest selection as one possible explanation for aggregate certainty patterns, rather than within-person change. Applying them here requires distinguishing expressed language from private certainty and disagreement from personal attack.

\subsection{Community Context in Online Moral Judgment}
\label{subsec:related-aita}

AITA research maps dilemma topics and aggregate judgments \cite{nguyen2022mapping,yudkin2025large,shi2025moral}, examines demographic and narrative correlates \cite{decandia2022socialnorms,giorgi2023author}, and categorizes commenters' reasoning \cite{xi2024morality}. More directly, Mellody studies comment valuation as consensus develops \cite{mellody2025consensus}. Goglia and Vega examine growing conversation networks \cite{goglia2024structure}; subsequent work analyzes judgment and language shifts around AITA's 18-hour verdict disclosure, noting participant selection and visibility as obstacles to causal interpretation \cite{goglia2026influence}.

Research on subreddit governance and norm interventions motivates attention to community context \cite{fiesler2018rules,matias2019norms}. Our AITAH comparison examines which reception and expression patterns recur, including how minority judgments are received around cross-posted stories. Shared narratives do not equalize audiences or exposure, and community differences cannot isolate verdict disclosure from moderation or membership. Rather than treating the communities as a natural experiment, we examine the scope of the AITA findings and the reception of two directions of moral dissent.
