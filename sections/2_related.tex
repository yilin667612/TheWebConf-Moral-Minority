\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Social Influence and the Reception of Minority Dissent}
\label{subsec:related-minority-reception}

Social influence research distinguishes the information people obtain from others from the social pressures surrounding agreement. Classic experiments show that individuals can align their responses with a unanimous majority even on relatively unambiguous perceptual tasks \cite{asch1956independence}. Informational influence concerns reliance on others as evidence, whereas normative influence concerns meeting social expectations and gaining acceptance \cite{deutsch1955normative,toelch2015conformity}. Influence also operates from minorities to majorities: a consistent minority can shift majority responses \cite{moscovici1969minority}, and a meta-analysis finds that minority influence is more evident on private, indirect measures than on public responses \cite{wood1994minority}. Public acceptance and influence on others' judgments are therefore distinct outcomes.

A complementary tradition examines how groups respond to people who disagree. Schachter's experiments linked persistent dissent to efforts to change the dissenter's position and eventual social rejection \cite{schachter1951deviation}. A replication recovered rejection and greater overall communication toward the deviant, but did not find a significant interaction between confederate role and discussion time \cite{wesselmann2014revisiting}. Attention to dissent thus need not imply acceptance, nor does it establish a uniform progression from engagement to exclusion. Meta-analytic evidence further indicates that consistently dissenting members face stronger rejection when their faction constitutes a smaller share of the group \cite{tata1996proportionate}.

These traditions motivate comparing minority reception across agreement levels while distinguishing reception from conformity or persuasion. Our numerical minority categories describe expressed judgments within a post, not demographic groups or participants' perceived social positions. High agreement identifies a strongly imbalanced distribution of judgments; it does not establish that commenters perceived the same distribution or experienced pressure to conform. We examine the evaluations and conversations surrounding dissent, rather than infer individual attitude change from aggregate patterns.

\subsection{Blame, Accountability, and the Expression of Dissent}
\label{subsec:related-selective-expression}

Moral judgments are not only assessments of conduct but also social acts whose legitimacy can be contested. Malle and colleagues characterize blame as a judgment that draws on information about norm violations, causality, intentionality, and reasons or preventability \cite{malle2014blame}. Their account of socially regulated blame emphasizes that public criticism imposes costs on the person criticized, the critic, and the community. Communities can therefore demand warrant for blame: critics may be expected to justify their judgments, and unfair criticism can itself become a target of criticism \cite{malle2022socially}. In online moral discussions, this perspective directs attention to two objects of evaluation: the OP's conduct and commenters' judgments of that conduct.

Complementary evidence shows that opposing moral evaluations need not be mirror images. Guglielmo and Malle find that blame differentiates more finely among an agent's mental states than praise does, with weaker evidence that blame is also more extreme \cite{guglielmo2019asymmetric}. Although defending someone against blame is not equivalent to praising them, these findings motivate examining directional asymmetries rather than assuming that opposing moral judgments are socially interchangeable.

These accounts motivate separating the direction of dissent from its minority status. Blaming dissent assigns fault to an OP whom most commenters defend, whereas defending dissent rejects blame endorsed by most commenters. Both challenge a prevailing view, but one calls for condemnation while the other disputes its legitimacy. The social regulation of blame provides a rationale for investigating whether these positions receive different responses; it does not establish in advance which will be more accepted. Nor does it imply that challenges to a blaming comment reflect fair scrutiny rather than simple disagreement. Research on moral rebels likewise finds that reactions can differ between participants implicated in a situation and uninvolved observers \cite{monin2008rejection}. Although that experimental distinction is not equivalent to OP versus other commenters, it motivates examining the source of responses as well as their content.

Accountability research offers a related account of how anticipated evaluation can shape expression. Tetlock and colleagues find that people may accommodate a known audience, consider multiple perspectives when the audience's views are unknown, or bolster positions to which they are already committed \cite{tetlock1989strategies}. Thus, demands for justification need not produce either conformity or more qualified expression in every case. Doubtful language is also associated with fewer recommendations in online news discussions \cite{evans2023doubt}. These studies motivate examining expressed certainty without equating linguistic confidence with private conviction, evidentiary quality, or persuasive success. Neither accountability nor blame theory directly predicts that blaming or defending dissent must always be more certain.

Anticipated evaluation may also shape who chooses to speak. Across three surveys, Matthes and colleagues find that opinion climate is associated with expression among individuals with low or moderate attitude certainty, but not among those with high certainty \cite{matthes2010spiral}. Neubaum and Kr\"amer show that anticipated personal attacks help explain differences in willingness to express minority opinions across online and offline settings \cite{neubaum2018sanctions}. More broadly, Settle's review identifies voluntary participation as a source of selection: expressed opinions may disproportionately represent people with strong views or low conflict avoidance \cite{settle2025promise}. Together, these studies motivate considering certainty-dependent selection into expression, rather than interpreting changes in aggregate expressed certainty solely as changes within individuals. Applying this account to moral discussion requires distinguishing disagreement from personal attack and expressed certainty from the attitude certainty measured in prior work.

\subsection{Evaluation and Conversational Engagement Online}
\label{subsec:related-online-engagement}

Platform feedback makes some aspects of reception observable, but different feedback measures capture different responses. Experiments show that prior ratings can affect subsequent evaluations \cite{muchnik2013social,glenski2017rating}, while research on Reddit voting highlights the role of local norms and rating practices \cite{graham2021sociomateriality}. Scores are therefore socially mediated evaluations rather than direct measures of agreement with a comment's substantive judgment. Research also distinguishes responding, disliking, and flagging as different reactions to objectionable comments \cite{kalch2017replying}. Most directly, Davis and Graham find that downvoted Reddit comments receive more replies and larger reply trees than upvoted comments \cite{davis2021emotional}. Negative evaluation and conversational attention can coexist.

Our study revisits that score--reply pattern using verdict alignment, rather than ratings, to identify minority judgments. It then asks what the additional attention contains. Direct replies can support a judgment, challenge it, or seek information; their frequency alone cannot distinguish these possibilities. Work on conversation structure further distinguishes broad participation from repeated exchanges among participants \cite{backstrom2013threads,choi2015reddit}, motivating separate examination of reply entry and subsequent conversational depth. Similarly, thread-level controversy research examines disagreement or mixed feedback surrounding an item \cite{hessel2019brewing}, whereas our comparison concerns how commenters on opposing sides of the same moral question are received. Combining judgment alignment, reply structure, and direct-response annotations allows us to examine the social reception of dissent without treating conversation as endorsement.

\subsection{Collective Moral Judgment in AITA Communities}
\label{subsec:related-aita}

AITA provides a setting for studying everyday morality through first-person narratives and explicit judgments. Prior work maps dilemma topics and aggregate moral evaluations \cite{nguyen2022mapping,yudkin2025large,shi2025moral}, examines demographic and narrative correlates of judgments \cite{decandia2022socialnorms,giorgi2023author}, and categorizes the reasoning expressed in comments \cite{xi2024morality}. These studies establish the range of moral situations and explanations represented in the community. Our focus is the reception of individual judgments that challenge a post's dominant verdict.

Recent research directly addresses consensus and disagreement within AITA. Mellody examines how the relative valuation of agreeing and dissenting comments varies with developing consensus \cite{mellody2025consensus}. Goglia and Vega examine the structure of growing conversation networks \cite{goglia2024structure}, while Goglia and colleagues analyze shifts in judgments and language around AITA's 18-hour verdict disclosure \cite{goglia2026influence}. The latter explicitly caution against causal interpretation because participant selection and comment visibility may also change.

We extend the AITA analysis to AITAH to examine whether reception patterns recur in another moral-discussion community. Research on subreddit governance and norm interventions shows why community context can matter for participation \cite{fiesler2018rules,matias2019norms}. However, differences between AITA and AITAH cannot isolate the effect of verdict disclosure from moderation, membership, or other contextual differences. The comparison therefore probes the scope of the AITA findings rather than treating the communities as a natural experiment. Across these literatures, our contribution is to connect agreement strength with the reception of two directions of moral dissent, distinguishing public evaluation, conversational response, and expressed certainty from unobserved conformity or persuasion.
