\section{Data and Methods}
\label{sec:data-method}

\subsection{Data Sources and Analytical Samples}
\label{subsec:dataset}

We study public moral discussions in r/AmItheAsshole (AITA), where an
original poster (OP) describes an interpersonal conflict and commenters
judge the OP's conduct. AITA is our primary setting. We use r/AITAH (AITAH),
which has a similar narrative-and-judgment format, as a cross-community
comparison.

\paragraph{Post and reply structure.}
Each discussion begins with a submission, or \emph{post}, whose title and
body present the OP's account. Comments attached directly to this post are
\emph{top-level comments}; these may state a verdict on the OP's conduct
and explain the reasoning behind it. Participants can also reply to an
existing comment, and those replies can receive further replies, forming
nested conversation branches. A \emph{direct reply} in our analysis is an
immediate child of a top-level verdict comment, whereas its \emph{reply
subtree} includes all subsequent descendants. The OP can participate in
these exchanges alongside other commenters. We distinguish judgments about
the original situation from responses to those judgments: retained
top-level verdict comments define the post's judgment distribution, while
their replies provide the material for analyzing conversational reception.
Direct-response annotation considers only immediate parent--reply pairs;
reply-tree measures additionally capture deeper exchanges.

\paragraph{Archive coverage.}
The processed source archives contain $871{,}754$ submissions and
$112{,}328{,}464$ comments from AITA, and $568{,}356$ submissions and
$36{,}123{,}353$ comments from AITAH. Their documented coverage begins in
June 2013 and March 2021, respectively, and extends through December 2025.
The main high-volume post cohort described below spans 2018--2025.
The records link submissions, comment text, extracted verdicts, scores,
timestamps, and parent--reply relationships. Content and user-overlap
comparisons use the aligned period from 17 March 2021 to 23 December 2025;
other analyses use their specified samples rather than assuming a shared
calendar filter.

\paragraph{Eligibility.}
We retain top-level comments containing a recognized verdict. The main
analytical cohort requires at least $200$ retained verdict comments per
post, yielding $28{,}107$ AITA and $8{,}217$ AITAH posts before agreement-band
and exact-tie exclusions. We retain judgments that assign or
reject fault on the OP's part, using the groups defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:method}, and exclude posts with an exact tie between
them from alignment-based comparisons. Nested replies are analyzed as
responses to judgments; they do not enter the post's verdict distribution.
The source processing excludes submissions with empty or removed bodies.
AutoModerator comments remain in the archive but receive no verdict label,
so they do not enter verdict counts. Structural reception queries select on
stored verdict labels without an additional author-identity filter and count
linked replies irrespective of whether their text is usable for annotation.
The direct-response sample instead requires usable post, parent, and reply
text and verifiable author identities, as specified below.

\subsection{Operationalizing Minority Moral Dissent}
\label{subsec:method}

\paragraph{Verdict extraction and grouping.}
We assign each comment the first recognized verdict expression in its text.
Short codes are matched case-sensitively with word boundaries, preventing
lowercase words such as \texttt{nah} and substrings such as \texttt{ESH}
in \texttt{FRESH} from being treated as verdicts. Full phrases, such as
\emph{not the asshole} and \emph{you're the asshole}, are matched
case-insensitively. When a comment contains multiple recognized expressions,
the first in textual order determines its verdict label.

Hypothetical forms are normalized as YWBTA to YTA and YWNBTA to NTA;
H-suffixed aliases in AITAH are mapped to their corresponding tags. We
then group labels by whether they assign fault to the OP:
$\mathrm{YA}=\{\text{YTA},\text{ESH}\}$ and
$\mathrm{NA}=\{\text{NTA},\text{NAH}\}$.
INFO and comments without a recognized verdict are excluded from verdict
counts. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-tags} lists the tags, aliases, and their
meanings.

\paragraph{Alignment and agreement.}
For a post $p$, let $N_{\mathrm{YA}}(p)$ and $N_{\mathrm{NA}}(p)$ count
retained top-level verdicts in each group. The retained verdict count is
$N(p)=N_{\mathrm{YA}}(p)+N_{\mathrm{NA}}(p)$, stored as
\texttt{YANA\_total}. We define the final observed \emph{YA share} as
\[
\rho(p)=\frac{N_{\mathrm{YA}}(p)}{N(p)}.
\]
Here, \emph{final} refers to the available archive, not an official
community-assigned verdict or a complete lifetime record. Throughout,
$\rho(p)$ denotes this final observed share; running or window-specific
proportions are identified separately.

The dominant verdict is $D(p)=\mathrm{YA}$ when $\rho(p)>0.5$ and
$D(p)=\mathrm{NA}$ when $\rho(p)<0.5$; exact ties are excluded. A verdict
comment is majority-aligned when it matches $D(p)$ and minority-aligned
otherwise. High-agreement posts satisfy $\rho(p)\le0.20$ or
$\rho(p)\ge0.80$; low-agreement posts satisfy
$0.40\le\rho(p)\le0.60$, excluding ties. Intermediate bands are omitted
from the main low/high comparison. Low-agreement posts provide a comparison
for our primary focus on minority judgments in high-agreement posts.

The distribution plots (Figures~\ref{fig:ya-share} and
\ref{fig:aitah-ya-share}) show the $200$-verdict cohort before exact-tie
exclusion, using one-percentage-point bins of final YA share. The endpoint
$\rho(p)=1$ is included in the final bin. Bars count posts; the right-axis
line divides the sum of stored $N(p)$ values in each bin by that bin's
post count. It therefore measures mean retained top-level verdicts per
post, not all comments or nested replies. The horizontal axis expresses
$\rho(p)$ as a percentage: $0$ and $100\%$ denote unanimous NA and YA,
respectively, and $50\%$ denotes an exact tie. Shading marks the
high-agreement bands.

\paragraph{Direction of dissent.}
Blaming dissent is a minority YA judgment in an NA-dominant post; defending
dissent is a minority NA judgment in a YA-dominant post. Each direction is
compared with majority comments in the corresponding post type. Comparing
the two directions therefore involves different post cohorts, not two
randomly assigned responses to the same story. Alignment and agreement
labels remain fixed to the final observed distribution, including in temporal
analyses; they do not measure what commenters perceived when posting.

\subsection{Reception and Direct Responses}
\label{subsec:method-reception}

\paragraph{Evaluation and conversational engagement.}
Comment score is the recorded net vote count: upvotes minus downvotes.
Direct-reply count measures immediate responses to a verdict comment.
Reply depth is the maximum number of reply levels beneath that comment,
with zero indicating no replies. Subtree score sums the scores of the
initiating comment and all observed replies in its subtree. Reply incidence
and thresholded engagement measures use the count or depth threshold
specified with each analysis.

We compare majority and minority reception across agreement levels (RQ1),
then compare blaming and defending dissent in high-agreement posts, each
with its corresponding majority baseline (RQ2). Means and proportions pool
eligible comments with equal comment weight; larger posts consequently
contribute more observations. Scores are the last values recorded in the
dataset, not scores measured at a common age after posting. Aggregate reply
measures use all recorded linked replies without an additional elapsed-time
cutoff. Neither measure is assumed to capture complete lifetime reception.
Missing or nonnumeric scores are excluded from score means rather than
imputed as zero; these comments remain eligible for structural reply measures.
For the full-cohort baseline, reply trees are reconstructed from the recorded
parent links within each post, and maximum depth is computed beneath each
retained verdict comment. This computation is checked against the original
recursive database traversal on selected comment subtrees.

For the arrival-hour comparison, we group parent verdict comments posted
within $[0,18)$ hours into one-hour bins and calculate pooled means, medians,
and empirical percentile ranges. High-agreement majority and minority
comments are kept separate; low-agreement verdict comments are pooled as
a reference distribution. Only parent arrival is restricted: scores and
direct replies retain the same archive-wide observation definitions.
Percentiles use linear interpolation between adjacent ordered observations.
In Figures~\ref{fig:comment-engagement-over-time} and
\ref{fig:aitah-arrival-reception}, columns show high-agreement majority,
high-agreement minority, and pooled low-agreement verdict comments.
Symmetric-log vertical axes are linear within $\pm1$ score point and
$\pm0.01$ replies, and logarithmic outside those ranges; ticks remain in
original units. The AITA and AITAH panels use matched axis ranges.
Display-axis transformations do not change the underlying estimates.

\paragraph{OP participation.}
\label{subsec:method-op-participation}
We separately describe OP participation using every eligible post in the
$200$-verdict cohort with an available submission author and a non-tied
final verdict distribution; no post subsampling is applied. This leaves
$26{,}997$ AITA and $8{,}211$ AITAH posts. A comment is attributed to the
OP when its recorded author matches the submission author. We exclude
submissions whose author is missing, \texttt{[deleted]}, or
\texttt{[removed]}, and omit AutoModerator comments. Participation counts
any recorded OP comment, whether top-level or nested and whether or not it
contains a verdict, over the full observed thread.

In bins of width $0.05$ in final YA share $\rho(p)$, OP presence is the
proportion of eligible posts with at least one matched comment. Shaded bands
are pointwise $95\%$ Wilson score intervals for this post-level proportion,
not bootstrap intervals. Conditional on OP presence, we measure elapsed
time from submission to the earliest recorded OP comment and summarize it
as under $1$ hour, $1$--$6$ hours, $6$--$24$ hours, or at least $24$ hours;
each post contributes once. The timing panel therefore has a different
denominator from the presence panel. These archive-based measures cannot
recover participation obscured by deleted author identities or missing
comments. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-op-participation} reports both figures.

\paragraph{Direct-response annotation.}
\label{subsec:method-rq2}
The annotation unit is a pair consisting of a top-level verdict comment and
one reply directly attached to it. The original submission provides context,
but the reply is classified relative to its parent comment, not relative to
the OP or the crowd's verdict. Categories are support, challenge,
information-seeking, and other. They describe communicative function rather
than sentiment: a polite rebuttal can be a challenge, while qualified
agreement can remain support. The prompt supplies no comment scores,
alignment labels, or agreement-level metadata. Category definitions and
mixed-response rules appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-rq2-annotation}.

The annotated analysis excludes OP participation to distinguish responses
by other commenters from engagement by the person being judged.
Pairs are excluded if either the parent or child was written by the OP,
if parent and child have the same author, or if authorship cannot be verified,
including missing or deleted identities. Author-ineligible draws are replaced
within their strata using a deterministic random candidate order.

Sampling is without replacement within each sample, using seeds
$2026090901$, $2026090902$, and $2026090903$, without post/parent caps or
forced disjointness across samples. Shared pairs are annotated separately
in each sample. Within-stratum proportions are unweighted; equal-stratum
summaries are not population-weighted prevalence estimates. Because sampling
is over pairs, posts and parents with more eligible replies can contribute
more observations. Overlapping samples do not constitute independent
discussion populations.
We compare response-category proportions across parent alignment, agreement
level, dissent direction, and community. Their denominator is labeled direct
replies, whereas reply incidence uses eligible parent comments, including
those receiving no replies. Thus, the proportion of replies that challenge a
judgment is distinct from the probability that a judgment receives a reply
or a challenge. This annotation does not, by itself, identify personal attacks.
Annotation uses prompt version \texttt{v0.3.3}, temperature $0$, and disabled
thinking, with individual requests processed at concurrency $10$. Successful
responses report the model alias \texttt{deepseek-flash}; the execution record
identifies the service as DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, but the alias is not an
immutable backend identifier. Each pair receives one of the four categories
and a rationale; all three completed samples have a final category for every
pair, so missing outputs are not absorbed into \emph{other}. Prompt development
and human review used $100$ author-verified pairs whose labels were revised
after inspecting disagreements. This is development-set review, not
independent held-out validation. Further execution and review details appear
in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-rq2-annotation}.

\subsection{Expressed Linguistic Certainty}
\label{subsec:method-certainty-contrasts}

We characterize how firmly minority judgments are expressed using a
four-level annotation scale: unresolved or conflicting judgments ($1$), a
leaning that allows an alternative ($2$), a qualified single judgment ($3$),
and a confidently asserted single judgment ($4$). The same criteria apply
to blaming and defending comments. This measures expressed linguistic
certainty, not private confidence or attitude strength. The prompt and
examples appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-annotation}. Verdict labels
still follow the first-match rule, including when the certainty annotation
identifies an unresolved judgment.

The aggregate certainty comparison for RQ2 uses the full observed period without
an upper elapsed-time cutoff, retaining minority comments with valid labels
in $\{1,2,3,4\}$. We calculate the proportions at each certainty level within
each post, then average these proportions with equal post weight. A post
without a valid minority certainty observation is excluded, not assigned zero.
Unlike pooled reception measures, these estimates give larger and smaller
contributing posts equal weight.

We summarize blaming-minus-defending differences in the proportion assigned
level $4$, with mean certainty as a complementary summary. Within each
community and agreement band, $95\%$ percentile intervals use $10{,}000$
bootstrap replicates that independently resample whole posts from each
directional cohort, using random seed $20260922$. Retaining each post's comments together preserves
within-post dependence; contrast intervals are calculated from replicate-level
differences. Full distributions, annotation-version checks, and first-$18$-hour
sensitivity comparisons appear in
Appendices~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty-comparison}
and~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty-window}. These are between-cohort comparisons,
not estimates of within-person change. Archive follow-up may differ across posts.

\paragraph{Certainty across relative comment position.}
Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-progress} complements the full-period comparison
with first-day profiles for RQ2 and RQ3. For each post, eligible verdict
comments arriving within the first $24$ hours are ordered by creation time,
with comment ID breaking timestamp ties, and divided into ten approximately
equal-count stages. For zero-based rank $r$ among $m$ eligible comments,
stage membership is $\lfloor 10r/m\rfloor$. The horizontal axis is relative
position in the observed first-day sequence, not clock time or the fraction
of the complete discussion lifetime.

Within each post and stage, we average the annotated certainty of minority
comments, then average these post-level means with equal post weight.
The sampling frame is the same $1{,}195$ annotated posts described above,
using stored final verdict shares and agreement assignments rather than
recomputing them from annotated comments. Five exact-tie AITA low-agreement
posts are excluded from directional comparisons. For the primary stage
analysis, we retain only posts with at least one minority comment with a
valid certainty label in each of the ten stages. Each curve therefore
compares the same post set throughout. Missing minority certainty is
undefined, not zero; posts missing any stage are excluded from that curve.

The resulting fixed cohorts contain $584$ posts and $92{,}716$ scored
minority comments. In blaming/defending order, post counts are $143/55$
(high) and $134/137$ (low) in AITA, and $49/8$ (high) and $28/30$ (low)
in AITAH. Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-progress} reports comment counts,
not post counts, in the same order. Pointwise $95\%$ intervals use
$2{,}000$ whole-post bootstrap replicates (seed $20260927$), resampling
within each fixed community--band--direction cohort and retaining each
post's complete stage sequence. These are not simultaneous bands or tests
of stage-specific directional differences or temporal trends.

The inclusion rule holds post composition constant but restricts the
estimand to discussions with scored minority expression throughout; it
can favor larger threads or those with more frequent minority comments.
It does not represent all annotated posts or discussions in which minority
expression disappears. For sensitivity comparison, we also retain
stage-specific available-post estimates without the all-stage requirement;
their contributing post sets can vary across stages.

These profiles describe the language of observed comments, not
longitudinal changes in the same individuals. They do not observe people
who considered commenting but remained silent, or establish that commenters
saw earlier judgments or responses.

\subsection{Participation Across Discussion Stages}
\label{subsec:method-temporal}

We distinguish elapsed time from progress through a comment sequence.
Elapsed time is measured from submission to comment arrival. Window-specific
minority share counts minority verdicts among verdicts arriving in a given
window; cumulative share includes all verdicts observed up to that time.
Blaming share is measured within NA-dominant posts and defending share within
YA-dominant posts. Both retain the final-distribution alignment labels.
The broad participation analysis uses eligible verdict comments rather than
requiring certainty annotations. For Figure~\ref{fig:minority-ratio-time},
high-agreement posts satisfy $N(p)\ge200$. Comments are assigned to hourly
bins $[h,h+1)$ for $h=0,\ldots,23$, using nonnegative elapsed times below
$24$ hours and excluding unavailable comment timestamps. At each hour end,
we divide cumulative minority-comment counts by cumulative retained verdict
counts pooled across the cohort. Larger posts therefore contribute more
comments; this is not a mean of within-post proportions. An empty hourly
bin adds no counts, leaving a previously defined cumulative share unchanged.
These curves are descriptive and have no uncertainty bands.

\paragraph{Relative comment position.}
Window-specific participation can also be summarized by relative comment
position using the stage construction in
Section~\ref{subsec:method-certainty-contrasts}. Within a stage, minority
share divides minority verdicts by all retained verdicts in that stage;
post-level shares are then averaged with equal post weight. Unlike certainty,
minority share is zero when a stage has verdict comments but no minority
comments; certainty is undefined in that case. The two measures therefore
need not describe the same contributing posts. Figure~\ref{fig:minority-ratio-time}
instead uses the cumulative, elapsed-time construction above.

These summaries describe the composition and language of observed comments,
not longitudinal changes in the same individuals. They do not observe people
who considered commenting but remained silent, or establish that commenters
saw earlier judgments or responses.

\subsection{Cross-Community Comparison and Shared Stories}
\label{subsec:method-community}

We apply the same verdict definitions and measures in AITAH to compare
minority reception, dissent direction, and expressed certainty.
Content and user-overlap comparisons use the aligned calendar period
specified in Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}; reception comparisons retain their
analysis-specific sampling frames and observation windows.
Community membership is an observed grouping variable, not an experimentally
assigned condition. The comparison does not isolate the effects of community
rules, participant composition, or topic selection.

To characterize content overlap, we use post embeddings from
\texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5} \citep{bge_embedding} and
\texttt{intfloat/e5-base-v2} \citep{wang2022text}, examining projections,
community-prediction performance, distributional distances, and topic
composition (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-content}). We describe shared users
and authors using intersection counts and Jaccard overlap, including activity-
and tenure-restricted cohorts (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-overlap}), and
summarize comment-arrival timing (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-timing}).

\paragraph{Cross-posted stories.}
We use cross-posted stories for contextual case studies rather than a
principal quantitative test of community differences. Candidate matches
require identical title-and-body text after Unicode NFKC normalization,
case folding, and whitespace normalization; empty, deleted, and removed
bodies are excluded. We identify $4{,}150$ distinct stories, defined by
unique normalized content, that appear in both communities. Of these,
$3{,}913$ have exactly one submission in each community, forming one-to-one
pairs. The remaining $237$ appear multiple times in at least one community
and allow multiple submission pairings.

Among the cross-community submission pairs, $1{,}871$ have an observed YA
share $\rho(p)$ in both communities. Submissions with no retained verdicts
have an undefined share; these values are not imputed. Requiring at least
$20$ retained verdicts in each submission leaves $107$ pairs in the updated
export. This case-selection threshold is separate from the $200$-verdict
threshold used in the main analyses. Within selected pairs, we examine
verdict distributions and individual parent--reply exchanges, keeping
aggregate verdict counts distinct from the scores of particular comments.
Matching story text does not match audiences, posting times, or exposure
to replies.
