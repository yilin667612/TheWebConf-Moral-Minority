\section{Data and Methods}
\label{sec:data-method}

We analyze public archives from r/AmItheAsshole (AITA) and r/AITAH (AITAH),
including posts, comments, replies, judgment labels, scores, timestamps, and reply structure.
We examine how minority judgments are expressed and received, and whether
replies introduce additional perspectives.

\subsection{Data and Measures}
\label{subsec:dataset}

A \emph{post} presents an interpersonal conflict described by its original
poster (OP). Throughout this paper, a \emph{comment} means a top-level
response to the post and a \emph{reply} means a direct response to a
comment. A \emph{reply thread} comprises these replies and any further
nested responses beneath the comment.
The archives cover posts published from June 2013 to December 2025 in AITA and from March 2021 to December 2025 in AITAH.
The posts with empty or removed bodies are excluded.

\paragraph{Judgments.}
A \emph{judgment} is a comment's expressed assessment of the OP's conduct.
We group judgments that assign fault to the OP as \emph{YA}: YTA
(``You're the Asshole'') and ESH (``Everyone Sucks Here,'' blaming both
parties). Judgments that do not assign fault to the OP form \emph{NA}:
NTA (``Not the Asshole'') and NAH (``No Assholes Here,'' blaming neither
party). We use the first recognized judgment in each comment, matching
short codes as case-sensitive whole words and full phrases regardless of
case. Hypothetical forms and equivalent variants map to the same categories;
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-tags} lists these forms.

Judgment counts include only comments assigned to these four categories;
INFO (requests for information), unrecognized comments, and AutoModerator
messages are excluded. For the reception analysis, we retain posts with
at least 200 such judgments. After these filters, the sample contains
28,107 AITA posts and 8,217 AITAH posts.

\paragraph{Majority, minority, and agreement.}
The \emph{dominant judgment} is the more frequent category, YA or NA,
among a post's retained judgments. \emph{Majority comments} express that
judgment; \emph{minority comments} express the opposite judgment.
We exclude posts with equal numbers of YA and NA judgments from comparisons
requiring this distinction. \emph{High agreement} means that at least
80\% of judgments favor one side. \emph{Low agreement} means that each
side accounts for 40--60\%. Intermediate levels
enter only analyses spanning the agreement range.

\emph{Blaming dissent} assigns fault to an OP whom the majority defends;
\emph{defending dissent} defends an OP whom the majority blames. Comparing
these directions therefore involves different sets of posts. These
classifications use the final observed judgment distribution and remain
fixed throughout temporal analyses. They do not represent the opinion
distribution visible to a commenter or an official community judgment.

\paragraph{Reception measures.}
\emph{Comment score} is the last recorded number of upvotes minus downvotes.
\emph{Reply count} is the number of replies to a comment.
\emph{Maximum reply depth} is the greatest number of response levels
beneath a comment: zero for no replies, one for replies without further
responses, and higher values for more deeply nested reply threads.
These measures use stored judgment labels and response links; unlike
annotation, they do not require usable reply text or verifiable authors.

For each measure, we first average comments within each post and judgment
group, then average across posts with equal weight. Missing groups are
excluded rather than assigned zero, and missing scores affect only score
averages. Groups can therefore include different sets of posts and are
not necessarily paired comparisons. Scores and reply measures use all
available archive observations, not a uniform follow-up period.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:dimensions} summarizes the dimensions and measures;
additional reception measures and comparisons by comment arrival hour
appear in Appendices~\ref{sec:appendix-aita-agreement-gradient}
and~\ref{sec:appendix-aita-arrival}.

\subsection{Reply Annotation}
\label{subsec:method-annotation}

\paragraph{Support and challenge.}
We use three annotated samples of 16,000 comment--reply pairs from
high-agreement AITA and AITAH posts, drawn separately from the reception
sample and excluding OP participation. Given the post as context, DeepSeek
labels each reply's stance toward its comment: \emph{support} endorses
the comment's position, \emph{challenge} disputes it,
\emph{information-seeking} requests clarification, and \emph{other}
covers remaining functions. Challenge denotes disagreement, not necessarily
personal attack. Proportions weight pairs equally and describe comments
receiving eligible replies, rather than all comments. Labels underwent
development-set review, not independent held-out validation.

\paragraph{Perspectives in replies.}
We analyze 7,023 eligible comment--reply pairs from 100 high-agreement
AITA posts, reusing their support/challenge labels. A \emph{perspective}
is an argumentative consideration represented by a category in a taxonomy
derived separately for each post. An LLM constructs this taxonomy and
labels each comment and reply independently; each text may receive
multiple categories or none. A reply adds a perspective when it receives
a category absent from its comment. This measures addition relative to
that comment, not novelty across the discussion, independently of
support/challenge. Within each post and majority/minority group,
proportions include all eligible replies, including those assigned no
category; groups without replies are omitted. Sampling, eligibility,
and annotation procedures appear in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-annotation-details}.

\subsection{Expressed Certainty}
\label{subsec:method-certainty}

We use comment-level certainty annotations from sampled high- and
low-agreement posts in the 200-judgment cohort. The scale captures
expressed language, not private confidence: very uncertain (1), somewhat
uncertain (2), somewhat certain (3), and very certain (4). The same
criteria apply across judgments. Sampling and outstanding validation
requirements are documented in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-annotation-details}.

We compare certainty distributions and the proportion at level 4 between
blaming and defending dissent over the full observed period, weighting
posts equally. These estimates describe the annotated sample, not
community-wide certainty. Estimation and sensitivity procedures appear
in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty-comparison}.

\paragraph{Certainty across discussion stages.}
For high-agreement AITA posts, we divide the first 24 hours of judgment
comments into ten chronological stages containing approximately equal
numbers of comments. We compare majority, blaming minority, and defending
minority comments using equal post weights. Each group retains a fixed
set of posts with scored comments in every stage. The curves therefore
describe successive comments in these selected discussions, not changes
within individuals. Stage construction and uncertainty estimation appear
in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-certainty-progress}.
