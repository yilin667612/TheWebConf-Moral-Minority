\subsection{The r/AmItheAsshole and r/AITAH datasets}
\label{subsec:dataset}

We study two Reddit communities devoted to moral judgment,
\emph{r/AmItheAsshole} (AITA) and \emph{r/AITAH} (AITAH). They differ in age
and in how they formalize collective judgment.
AITA, created in 2013, follows a more procedural model: a bot tallies top-level
verdict comments and assigns the post an official verdict flair approximately
$18$ hours after submission. AITAH, created in 2021 as a more permissive
alternative, has no corresponding deadline or official verdict flair and
applies looser rules to admissible conflicts and post formatting. This
difference motivates the institutional comparison described in
\S\ref{subsec:method}. As of September 2026, AITA reports $2.8$M weekly visitors
and approximately $130$K weekly contributions, while AITAH reports $1.5$M
weekly visitors and $60$K weekly contributions.

AITAH is not the only spin-off of AITA, but it is the only usable comparison.
The other variants fail one of three requirements. \emph{r/AITA\_Relationships}
and \emph{r/AITARelationship} exist because relationship posts are barred from
AITA, so each covers a restricted slice of the domain rather than the whole of
it. \emph{r/AmITheAngel} and \emph{r/AmITheDevil} are commentary communities
built around AITA content rather than judgment communities in their own right,
and do not run the verdict-tag convention. The remaining variants, such as
\emph{r/AITH}, do use the convention but are smaller by orders of magnitude in
both visitors and contributions, leaving too few high-volume posts to estimate
a crowd verdict. AITAH alone matches AITA in scale, in scope, and in tag
vocabulary while differing in the institutional rule we want to vary.

\subsubsection{Verdicts and crowd judgment.}
An original poster (OP) submits a moral dilemma from their own life, and other
users respond in top-level comments that open with one of seven verdict tags
shared by the two communities (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-tags}). Six of the
tags assign blame, and we group them into two moral stances toward the OP: the
\textbf{exculpating} group
$\mathrm{NA}=\{\text{NTA},\text{YWNBTA},\text{NAH}\}$ and the
\textbf{inculpating} group
$\mathrm{YA}=\{\text{YTA},\text{YWBTA},\text{ESH}\}$. The hypothetical forms
\emph{YWNBTA} and \emph{YWBTA} apply to an action the OP has not yet taken, but
point the blame in the same direction as \emph{NTA} and \emph{YTA}. AITAH's
H-suffixed aliases, such as \emph{NTAH} and \emph{YTAH}, are mapped to their
unsuffixed counterparts. Only \emph{INFO} is excluded, as it requests
additional information rather than stating a judgment.

We retain only \emph{top-level verdict comments}, defined as direct replies to
a post that contain one of the retained judgment tags. For each post $p$,
we define the \emph{YA share}
\[
\rho(p)=\frac{\text{number of retained YA verdicts on }p}
{\text{number of retained verdicts on }p}.
\]
The dominant verdict is $D(p)=\mathrm{YA}$ when $\rho(p)\ge0.5$ and
$D(p)=\mathrm{NA}$ when $\rho(p)<0.5$.
Unless otherwise noted, analyses are restricted to \emph{high-volume} posts
with at least $200$ retained top-level verdict comments. We relax this
threshold for the paired duplicate-post analysis in
\S\ref{subsec:res-duplicates}, because matched posts rarely reach it in both
communities.

For the language analyses, we additionally use an annotated subset of roughly
one thousand high-volume threads per community. Each top-level verdict comment
in this subset carries a \emph{certainty} score on a four-point ordinal scale,
assigned by a language model prompted with the instructions and worked examples
reproduced in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-annotation}. A score of $4$ denotes a
blunt and assertive judgment, such as ``NTA. That's on her.'' A score of $1$
denotes a hedged or conditional judgment, such as ``YTA if you had the money,
otherwise not.'' The scale measures how firmly a verdict is asserted, not which
verdict it is, so certainty is comparable across the NA and YA groups. We
assign each annotated thread to an agreement category using all retained verdict
comments on the post, rather than only the annotated comments.

\subsection*{Cross-community setup}

AITA spans the full archive period, whereas AITAH appears only from 2021 onward.
We therefore use two temporal scopes. Within-community descriptive and trend
analyses use all available years for each community. Direct comparisons between
AITA and AITAH use the common window from 17 March 2021 to 23 December 2025,
when both communities are present in the archive. This aligned window reduces
confounding from AITA's longer history and period-specific changes.

\texttt{intfloat/e5-base-v2} \citep{wang2022text}.

\subsubsection{Shared users.}
Within the aligned window, we identify accounts active in both communities and,
separately, authors who submit posts to both. We report intersection counts and
Jaccard overlap for the full user and author sets. To assess sensitivity to
transient accounts, we repeat the user-level calculation after restricting the
sample to users with at least $10$ activities, at least $100$ activities, at
least $30$ days of observed tenure, and at least one year of observed tenure.

For each shared user, we compute the share of activity occurring in AITAH, the
community in which the user is first observed, the interval between first
appearances in the two communities, posting and commenting rates, mean comment
score, top-level comment rate, daily activity profile, and observed tenure.

\subsubsection{Comment timing.}
The two communities also accumulate comments on a similar schedule, which
matters for the threshold analysis in \S\ref{subsec:method}. The median post
reaches half of its top-level comments within $7.3$ hours in AITA and $6.7$
hours in AITAH, and $90\%$ of them within $17.7$ and $20.1$ hours. AITA's
verdict flair therefore appears at roughly the point at which a typical post
has already collected most of the top-level comments it will ever receive,
which bounds how much of the data the flair can plausibly affect.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-timing} reports the full distributions.

\subsubsection{Duplicate posts.}
We identify cross-community duplicates by exact text matching rather than
semantic similarity. We concatenate each post's title and body, apply Unicode NFKC normalization and case folding, collapse consecutive whitespace characters into a single space, and remove leading and
trailing whitespace. Posts with empty bodies or bodies marked as [deleted] or [removed] are excluded. Cross-community duplicates are identified by matching SHA-256 hashes of the normalized text.

After text normalization,

A pair is included in the paired analysis only when the YA share can be computed
for both submissions. This design holds the submitted text fixed when comparing
crowd judgments across communities. As noted above, the $200$-verdict threshold
is not applied to this analysis.



\subsection{Analytical dimensions and outcome measures}
\label{subsec:method}

Table~\ref{tab:dimensions} summarizes the four analytical dimensions and the
comment-level outcome measures.


\subsubsection*{(1) Majority versus minority: alignment with the crowd}
A top-level verdict comment is classified as \textbf{majority} when its verdict
group matches $D(p)$ and as \textbf{minority} otherwise. This is the primary
comment-level comparison.

\subsubsection*{(2) High-agreement versus low-agreement posts}
A post is \textbf{high-agreement} when $\rho(p)\le 0.20$ or
$\rho(p)\ge 0.80$, so that one verdict group has at least a four-to-one
advantage. A post is \textbf{low-agreement} when
$0.40\le\rho(p)\le 0.60$. Posts between these intervals are excluded from this
comparison.

\subsubsection*{(3) Blaming versus defending dissent}
Minority comments are divided by the direction of disagreement.
\textbf{Blaming} dissent is a YA judgment on an exculpating-dominant post.
\textbf{Defending} dissent is an NA judgment on an inculpating-dominant post.
Each type is compared with majority comments from posts with the same dominant
verdict, thereby holding the post-level judgment context fixed.

\subsubsection*{(4) AITA versus AITAH: community comparison}
We conduct each analysis separately in AITA and AITAH before comparing the
resulting patterns. Direct community comparisons use the aligned temporal window
defined above.

\paragraph{The 18-hour rule.}
AITA's official verdict flair introduces a threshold within each post's life
that has no counterpart in AITAH. For every top-level verdict comment, we
compute elapsed time since submission and estimate whether the minority share
and the certainty of minority comments change discontinuously at $18$ hours,
fitting a time trend together with an indicator for the post-threshold period
so that a jump at the boundary is separated from the gradual drift across the
window \textbf{[TODO: specify the functional form of the trend, the estimation
window, and the time binning]}. We apply the same threshold in AITAH as a placebo comparison
for changes associated with the ordinary aging of a discussion.

This analysis is observational. Thread age may coincide with audience turnover,
unequal opportunities for vote accumulation, and selection among late
commenters. We therefore do not interpret a threshold-associated change as the
causal effect of the verdict flair alone.

\paragraph{Outcome measures.}
Comment score measures net approval, whereas direct replies and reply depth
measure engagement. Thread score aggregates the scores of the verdict comment
and its complete reply subtree. Certainty measures the assertiveness of verdict
language in the annotated subset. Score and direct replies are computed for all
eligible comments, and certainty is computed for the annotated subset. Because
recursive traversal of every reply tree is computationally expensive, reply
depth and thread score are computed on a uniform random sample of posts; the
sample size is reported with the corresponding analyses.
