\section{Between-Subjects Survey Experiment}
\label{sec:method-survey}
We conducted a between-subjects survey experiment to examine how readers respond to discussions that include a minority judgment (\textbf{RQ4}). We compared exposure to two majority-aligned comment threads with exposure to one majority-aligned and one minority-aligned thread, measuring readers' judgments, perceived comment influence, and consideration of alternative perspectives.

\subsection{Survey Design}
\label{subsec:survey-design}

\paragraph{Design and stimuli}
Participants were randomly assigned to one of four high-agreement AITA posts (two with a majority \textit{YA} judgment and two with a majority \textit{NA} judgment) and one of two comment-display conditions. The posts concerned financial obligations between friends, parenting, punctuality, and workplace conduct. Figure~\ref{fig:survey-procedure} in Appendix~\ref{app:survey} illustrates the design and procedure.

In the \textit{Majority--Majority} condition (MM), participants saw the two highest-scoring majority comments. In the \textit{Majority--Minority} condition (Mm), the highest-scoring minority comment replaced the second majority comment. Within each scenario, the post and Comment~1 were held constant; only Comment~2 and its reply thread varied. The manipulation therefore combined thread content, replies, and observed scores.

The Reddit-style interface retained the original wording, scores, and \textit{YA}/\textit{NA} judgments. Replies were nested up to four levels, initially collapsed, and expandable via \textit{View replies}. Usernames were replaced with generic labels; majority/minority labels and agreement percentages were omitted.

\paragraph{Participants and procedure}

Participants were recruited through Prolific, resulting in a final
sample of 80 participants (40 male and 40 female), aged 19--58 years
($M_{\mathrm{age}} = 34.10$, $SD = 9.27$). Most participants were
aged 25--34 years (55.0\%). The sample was ethnically diverse
(47.5\% White, 18.8\% Black, 18.8\% Asian, and 15.0\% mixed, other,
or undisclosed) and included residents of 23 countries. Participants

\paragraph{Measures}
Moral judgment, the primary outcome, used an eight-point scale from 1 (Definitely YA) to 8 (Definitely NA), with four certainty levels in each direction. Each selected response thus encoded both judgment direction and certainty. We recoded certainty from 1 (least certain) to 4 (most certain), assigning the two innermost responses to 1 and the two endpoints to 4. This reader-selected certainty differs from the linguistic certainty annotated in comments. Per-comment influence, agreement, and perspective consideration used five-point scales (1--5). Participants also identified which comment contributed more to their judgment (Comment~1, Comment~2, both equally, or neither). Three seven-point items assessed discussion-level perspective broadening: considering both sides, encountering new considerations, and engaging with reasons supporting an alternative judgment. Display preference contrasted two highly upvoted comments supporting the same judgment with two representing opposing judgments, plus a neither option.

Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results} summarizes responses across the four scenarios, grouped by reported judgment alignment. We focus on observed means; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:survey-alignment-tests} reports all 16 unadjusted $p$-values from two-sided Welch's independent-samples $t$-tests as exploratory summaries, not confirmatory evidence across the multiple comparisons. The results describe responses to these four posts and their selected threads; they do not establish a general effect across posts.

Among readers whose reported judgments agreed with the majority, mean certainty was lower in Mm than in MM (2.61 vs.\ 3.43); among those agreeing with the minority, the observed ordering was reversed (2.89 vs.\ 2.30). These comparisons group readers by a judgment measured once after exposure; they do not estimate within-person change or causal effects within judgment groups.

In both judgment groups, mean discussion-level perspective broadening and perspective consideration for each comment were higher in Mm than in MM. Agreement with and perceived influence of Comment~2 showed different patterns: both were lower in Mm among majority-aligned readers but higher among minority-aligned readers. Agreement with the shared Comment~1 was similar across conditions, while its perceived influence was higher in Mm in both groups. Participants in both conditions most often preferred seeing one comment from each side (MM: 67.5\%; Mm: 57.5\%).

For \textbf{RQ4}, Mm participants reported broader consideration of perspectives in both judgment groups, while certainty and responses to the replacement comment varied with judgment alignment. These observations are limited to four selected posts, whose topics and comment quality vary. Because the manipulation combined comment content, replies, and scores, the patterns cannot be attributed to minority status alone. They motivate further study rather than establish general effects, changes in judgment, or better reasoning.
