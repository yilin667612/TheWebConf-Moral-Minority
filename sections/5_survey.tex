\section{Reader Responses to Minority Dissent}
\label{sec:method-survey}
Score-based comment displays may give greater visibility to majority judgments, making minority judgments and their alternative moral reasons less prominent.
To explore how readers respond when exposed to minority views, we conducted a survey experiment. We compared a \textit{Highly Scored} display containing two majority comments with a \textit{Balanced} display containing one majority comment and one minority comment. 
\lx{We examine whether readers shown a Balanced display report greater consideration of alternative moral reasons than those shown a Highly Scored display. We also compare judgment certainty to examine whether exposure to competing judgments is accompanied by firmer or more tentative final judgments (RQ3).}

\subsection{Survey Design}
\label{subsec:survey-design}

\runinheading{Design and stimuli}
We conducted a between-subjects survey experiment. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four high-agreement AITA posts (two with a majority \textit{YA} judgment and two with a majority \textit{NA} judgment) and one of two comment-display conditions. The posts concerned financial obligations, parenting, punctuality, and workplace conduct. Appendix~\ref{app:survey} provides details of the survey procedure and posts.

In the \textit{Highly Scored} condition, participants saw the two highest-scoring comments--this corresponds to the default sorting method on the ATIA community. In all of the four posts both comments with highest-score are also of the majority judgment. In the \textit{Balanced} condition, the highest-scoring minority comment replaced the second majority comment. For each post, the post content and Comment~1 were identical across conditions; only the comment 2 and its reply thread varied. 

Participants viewed the post and comments in a Reddit-style interface
that preserved their original wording and displayed scores and
\textit{YA}/\textit{NA} judgments. Reply threads were nested up to
four levels, initially collapsed, and expandable via \textit{View
replies}. The interface did not explicitly identify comments as
majority or minority or show the overall distribution of judgments.
Screenshots of the survey interface are provided in Supplementary~\ref{app:survey-interface}.

\runinheading{Participants}
We recruited 80 participants through Prolific, with English fluency
as an eligibility requirement. Participants ranged in age from 19 to 58 years (40 female and 40 male) and resided across 23 countries;
detailed demographic information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:survey}. Participants completed the study in an average of 9 minutes and received €1 as compensation. 

\runinheading{Measures}
To assess whether the displays broadened readers' access to alternative moral reasons, we measured their self-reported consideration at both the discussion and comment levels. 
At the discussion level, participants completed three
seven-point measures reflecting on the discussion (1=strongly disagree; 7=strongly agree):
\textit{New reasons}, assessing whether it raised moral considerations that had not initially occurred to them; \textit{Multiple sides}, assessing whether it prompted them to consider more than one side; and \textit{Alternative judgments}, assessing whether they considered why someone might reach a different conclusion. 
At the comment level, participants rated each comment on three five-point measures (1=not at all; 5=very strongly). \textit{New reasons} assessed whether the comment prompted consideration of a previously unconsidered moral reason or
perspective. \textit{Influence} and \textit{Agreement} assessed,
respectively, how influential participants found the comment and how
strongly they agreed with it. 

We measured judgment certainty using participants' responses on an
eight-point bipolar scale ranging from 1 (\textit{Definitely YA}) to 8 (\textit{Definitely NA}). 
We analyzed judgment direction separately and recoded responses into
four certainty levels: responses nearest the midpoint (4 and 5)
indicated the lowest certainty, whereas endpoint responses (1 and 8)
indicated the highest certainty.

Participants also reported \textit{Relative contribution} by indicating
whether Comment~1, Comment~2, both comments equally, or neither comment
contributed most to their final judgment. Finally,
\textit{Display preference} asked participants to select
\textit{Highly Scored}, \textit{Balanced}, or no preference. The full
wording and response options for all survey measures are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:survey}.

Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results-pooled} compares survey responses between the \textit{Highly Scored} and \textit{Balanced} conditions using all 40 participants in each condition.

\runinheading{Discussion-level reflection.}
Panel~(b) shows higher mean ratings in
the \textit{Balanced} condition for all three discussion-level items:
considering multiple sides (4.98 vs.\ 4.35), considering new reasons
(4.63 vs.\ 4.05), and considering why someone might reach an alternative
judgment (5.08 vs.\ 3.85). The largest difference was observed for the
alternative-judgment item, which was also the comparison marked as
statistically significant in the figure ($p<.01$). 

\runinheading{Comment-level evaluations.}
Panel~(c) reports perceived influence,
agreement, and new moral considerations separately for comment 1 and comment 2.
comment 1 was identical across the two conditions, whereas comment 2 represented the
majority-aligned comment in \textit{Highly Scored} and the
minority-aligned comment in \textit{Balanced}.

For the shared comment 1, perceived influence was higher in
\textit{Balanced} than in \textit{Highly Scored} (2.83 vs.\ 2.28),
whereas agreement was nearly identical across conditions
(3.90 vs.\ 3.95). Participants in \textit{Balanced} also gave comment 1 a
higher mean rating for introducing new moral considerations
(2.50 vs.\ 1.98).

For comment 2, perceived influence was slightly lower in \textit{Balanced}
than in \textit{Highly Scored} (1.95 vs.\ 2.20). The clearest
difference concerned agreement: participants agreed less with the
minority-aligned comment 2 in \textit{Balanced} than with the majority-aligned
comment 2 in \textit{Highly Scored} (2.78 vs.\ 4.03, $p<.001$). Nevertheless,
the mean rating for comment 2 introducing new moral considerations was higher
in \textit{Balanced} (2.48 vs.\ 2.00).

\runinheading{Judgment certainty.}
Panel~(a) shows lower mean judgment certainty in \textit{Balanced} than
in \textit{Highly Scored} (2.68 vs.\ 3.15, $p=.037$). This result suggests that exposure to comments representing opposing
positions was associated with lower reported certainty in participants'
final judgments.

\runinheading{Relative comment contribution and preferred display.}
Beyond the plotted measures, readers more often select comment 1 as contributing
most to their judgment in Balanced than in Highly Scored
($50\%$ versus $20\%$). comment 2 is selected by $15\%$ versus $10\%$, both equally
by $10\%$ versus $30\%$, and neither by $25\%$ versus $40\%$. 
Most participants prefer one comment from each side in both Highly Scored
($67.5\%$) and Balanced ($57.5\%$), although fewer report equal
contribution from both comments.

\runinheading{Exploratory patterns by judgment alignment.}
Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results} in Appendix further separates participants according to whether their final judgment aligned with the majority or minority
position. 
The descriptive certainty pattern differed across these
groups. Majority-aligned participants reported lower certainty in
\textit{Balanced} than in \textit{Highly Scored} (2.61 vs.\ 3.43),
whereas minority-aligned participants reported higher certainty in
\textit{Balanced} (2.89 vs.\ 2.30).
Discussion-level ratings
were generally higher in \textit{Balanced} within both alignment groups,
particularly for considering alternative judgments. 
Comment-level ratings of new moral considerations were also higher in
\textit{Balanced} in most subgroup comparisons.
Agreement ratings largely reflected the match between participants'
final judgment and the judgment expressed by each comment.
Majority-aligned participants reported strong agreement with the shared
majority comment (Comment~1) in both conditions and with Comment~2
when it also expressed the majority judgment in \textit{Highly Scored}.
Minority-aligned participants reported their strongest agreement with
the minority comment presented as Comment~2 in \textit{Balanced}.
These patterns are
exploratory, particularly given the small minority-aligned subgroups
($n=10$ in \textit{Highly Scored} and $n=9$ in \textit{Balanced}).



For \textbf{RQ3}, readers in Balanced report lower mean judgment certainty
and higher mean consideration ratings than readers in Highly Scored,
despite similar proportions of majority-aligned judgments. 
Among the three discussion-level items, the largest observed mean difference between conditions concerned readers’ reported consideration of reasons for an alternative judgment.
Minority influence theory offers a possible
explanation for this separation: encountering dissent may broaden the
alternatives considered without requiring adoption of the dissenting
position \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. Research on
disagreement and argument repertoire supports examining engagement with
reasons for opposing judgments as an outcome distinct from agreement
\cite{price2002disagreement,cappella2002argument}. Our findings identify
differences in judgment certainty and reported consideration between the
displays that the distribution of judgment alignment alone would miss.
