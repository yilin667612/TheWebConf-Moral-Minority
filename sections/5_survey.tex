\section{Reader Responses to Minority Dissent}
\label{sec:method-survey}
Online discussions may give greater visibility to majority opinions, while minority judgments offering alternative moral reasons may receive less attention. To explore how readers respond when exposed to minority views, we conducted a survey experiment. We compared a \textit{Highly Scored} display containing two majority comments with a \textit{Balanced} display containing one majority comment and one minority comment. 
\lx{We examine whether readers shown a Balanced display report greater consideration of alternative moral reasons than those shown a Highly Scored display. We also compare judgment certainty to examine whether exposure to competing judgments is accompanied by firmer or more tentative final judgments (RQ3).}

\subsection{Survey Design}
\label{subsec:survey-design}

\runinheading{Design and stimuli}
We conducted a between-subjects survey experiment. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four high-agreement AITA posts (two with a majority \textit{YA} judgment and two with a majority \textit{NA} judgment) and one of two comment-display conditions. The posts concerned financial obligations, parenting, punctuality, and workplace conduct. Appendix~\ref{app:survey} provides details of the survey procedure and scenarios.

In the \textit{Highly Scored} condition, participants saw the two highest-scoring majority comments. In the \textit{Balanced} condition, the highest-scoring minority comment replaced the second majority comment. Within each scenario, the post and Comment~1 were held constant; only Comment~2 and its reply thread varied. The manipulation therefore combined thread content, replies, and observed scores.

Participants viewed the post and comments in a Reddit-style interface
that preserved their original wording and displayed scores and
\textit{YA}/\textit{NA} judgments. Reply threads were nested up to
four levels, initially collapsed, and expandable via \textit{View
replies}. The interface did not explicitly identify comments as
majority or minority or show the overall distribution of judgments.

\runinheading{Participants and procedure}
We recruited 80 participants through Prolific, with English fluency
as an eligibility requirement. Participants ranged in age from 19 to 58 years (40 female and 40 male) and resided across 23 countries;
detailed demographic information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:survey}. Participants completed the study in an average of 9 minutes and received €1 as compensation. The study was approved by the human research ethics review body of *** (Project No. ***). All participants provided informed consent before participating in the study.

\runinheading{Measures}
Participants reported their moral judgment and judgment certainty using an eight-point bipolar scale ranging from 1 (Definitely YA) to 8 (Definitely NA). We analyzed judgment direction separately and recoded response strength as certainty, from 1 (least certain) to 4 (most certain). 

At the discussion level, participants completed three seven-point measures of their overall reflection on the two-comment discussion: \textit{Multiple sides}, assessing whether the
discussion prompted them to consider more than one side;
\textit{New reasons}, assessing whether it raised considerations that
had not initially occurred to them; and \textit{Alternative
judgments}, assessing whether they reflected on why someone might
reach a different conclusion.

At the comment level, participants rated each comment on three
five-point measures: \textit{Influence}, \textit{Agreement}, and
\textit{New reasons}, capturing respectively how influential they
found the comment, how strongly they agreed with it, and whether it
prompted them to consider a previously unconsidered perspective. Participants also reported \textit{Relative
contribution} by indicating whether Comment~1, Comment~2, both
equally, or neither contributed more to their final judgment.
Finally, the \textit{Display preference} measure asked participants
to select \textit{Highly Scored}, \textit{Balanced}, or no
preference. The full wording and response options for all survey
measures are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:survey}.

Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results-pooled} compares survey responses between the \textit{Highly Scored} and \textit{Balanced} conditions using all 40 participants in each condition.

\runinheading{Judgment certainty.}
Panel~(a) shows lower mean judgment certainty in \textit{Balanced} than
in \textit{Highly Scored} (2.68 vs.\ 3.15, $p=.037$). This result suggests that exposure to comments representing opposing
positions was associated with lower reported certainty in participants'
final judgments.

\runinheading{Discussion-level consideration of alternative views.}
Panel~(b) shows higher mean ratings in
the \textit{Balanced} condition for all three discussion-level items:
considering multiple sides (4.98 vs.\ 4.35), considering new reasons
(4.63 vs.\ 4.05), and considering why someone might reach an alternative
judgment (5.08 vs.\ 3.85). The largest difference was observed for the
alternative-judgment item, which was also the comparison marked as
statistically significant in the figure ($p<.01$). 

\runinheading{Responses to individual comments.}
Panel~(c) reports perceived influence,
agreement, and new moral considerations separately for C1 and C2.
C1 was identical across the two conditions, whereas C2 represented the
majority-aligned comment in \textit{Highly Scored} and the
minority-aligned comment in \textit{Balanced}.

For the shared C1 comment, perceived influence was higher in
\textit{Balanced} than in \textit{Highly Scored} (2.83 vs.\ 2.28),
whereas agreement was nearly identical across conditions
(3.90 vs.\ 3.95). Participants in \textit{Balanced} also gave C1 a
higher mean rating for introducing new moral considerations
(2.50 vs.\ 1.98).

For C2, perceived influence was slightly lower in \textit{Balanced}
than in \textit{Highly Scored} (1.95 vs.\ 2.20). The clearest
difference concerned agreement: participants agreed less with the
minority-aligned C2 in \textit{Balanced} than with the majority-aligned
C2 in \textit{Highly Scored} (2.78 vs.\ 4.03, $p<.001$). Nevertheless,
the mean rating for C2 introducing new moral considerations was higher
in \textit{Balanced} (2.48 vs.\ 2.00).

\runinheading{Relative comment contribution and preferred display.}
Beyond the plotted measures, readers more often select C1 as contributing
most to their judgment in Balanced than in Highly Scored
($50\%$ versus $20\%$). C2 is selected by $15\%$ versus $10\%$, both equally
by $10\%$ versus $30\%$, and neither by $25\%$ versus $40\%$. 
Most participants prefer one comment from each side in both Highly Scored
($67.5\%$) and Balanced ($57.5\%$), although fewer report equal
contribution from both comments.

\runinheading{Exploratory patterns by judgment alignment.}
Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results} further separates participants according to whether their final judgment aligned with the majority or minority
position. Among majority-aligned participants, judgment certainty was lower in \textit{Balanced} than in \textit{Highly Scored}
(2.61 vs.\ 3.43), whereas the descriptive pattern was reversed among
minority-aligned participants (2.89 vs.\ 2.30). Discussion-level ratings
were generally higher in \textit{Balanced} within both subgroups,
particularly for considering alternative judgments. Comment-level agreement largely followed participants' judgment alignment:
majority-aligned participants agreed strongly with C1 in both conditions
and with C2 when it was majority-aligned in \textit{Highly Scored},
whereas minority-aligned participants reported the strongest agreement
with C2 when it was minority-aligned in \textit{Balanced}. Ratings of
new moral considerations were also descriptively higher in
\textit{Balanced} across most subgroup comparisons. These patterns are
exploratory, particularly given the small minority-aligned subgroups
($n=10$ in \textit{Highly Scored} and $n=9$ in \textit{Balanced}).

These exploratory comparisons concern four selected scenarios; reported
Welch tests are two-sided and use unadjusted $p$-values. The figure's confidence intervals
use 20,000 bootstrap resamples of participants within each condition and
reported-judgment group, retaining each participant's responses together.

For \textbf{RQ3}, readers in Balanced report lower mean judgment certainty
and higher mean consideration ratings than readers in Highly Scored,
despite similar proportions of majority-aligned judgments. 
Among the three discussion-level items, the largest observed mean difference between conditions concerned readers’ reported consideration of reasons for an alternative judgment.
Minority influence theory offers a possible
explanation for this separation: encountering dissent may broaden the
alternatives considered without requiring adoption of the dissenting
position \cite{nemeth1986differential,nemeth1987divergent}. Research on
disagreement and argument repertoire supports examining engagement with
reasons for opposing judgments as an outcome distinct from agreement
\cite{price2002disagreement,cappella2002argument}. Our findings identify
differences in judgment certainty and reported consideration between the
displays that the distribution of judgment alignment alone would miss.
