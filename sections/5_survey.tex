\section{Reader Responses to Minority Dissent}
\label{sec:method-survey}
We examine how readers respond when a minority comment thread is presented
alongside a majority thread, compared with exposure to two majority threads.
We ask whether readers in these conditions differ in their certainty about
their own judgments, their agreement with the comments, and the influence
they attribute to each comment. We also compare the new moral considerations
and the range of considerations they report, examining readers' responses
to dissent beyond agreement with its position (\textbf{RQ4}).

\subsection{Survey Design}
\label{subsec:survey-design}

\runinheading{Design and stimuli}
We conducted a between-subjects survey experiment. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four high-agreement AITA posts (two with a majority \textit{YA} judgment and two with a majority \textit{NA} judgment) and one of two comment-display conditions. The posts concerned financial obligations between friends, parenting, punctuality, and workplace conduct. Appendix~\ref{app:survey} provides details of the survey procedure and scenarios.

In the \textit{Two Majority} condition, participants saw the two highest-scoring majority comments. In the \textit{Majority + Minority} condition, the highest-scoring minority comment replaced the second majority comment. Within each scenario, the post and Comment~1 were held constant; only Comment~2 and its reply thread varied. The manipulation therefore combined thread content, replies, and observed scores.

The Reddit-style interface retained the original wording, scores, and \textit{YA}/\textit{NA} judgments. Replies were nested up to four levels, initially collapsed, and expandable via \textit{View replies}. Usernames were replaced with generic labels; majority/minority labels and agreement percentages were omitted.

\runinheading{Participants and procedure}
We recruited 80 participants through the online platform Prolific, with fluency in English as an eligibility criterion. Participants were aged 19–58 years (M = 34.10, SD = 9.27), with 40 identifying as male and 40 as female. Most participants were aged 25–34 (55.0\%). Participants completed the study in an average of 9 minutes and received €1 as compensation.

\runinheading{Measures}
Moral judgment, the primary outcome, used an eight-point scale from 1 (Definitely YA) to 8 (Definitely NA), with four certainty levels in each direction. Each selected response thus encoded both judgment direction and certainty. We recoded certainty from 1 (least certain) to 4 (most certain), assigning the two innermost responses to 1 and the two endpoints to 4. This reader-selected certainty differs from the linguistic certainty annotated in comments. Per-comment influence, agreement, and ratings of \emph{new moral considerations} used five-point scales (1--5). Participants also identified which comment contributed more to their judgment (Comment~1, Comment~2, both equally, or neither). Three seven-point items assessed the discussion-level \emph{range of moral considerations}: considering both sides, encountering new considerations, and engaging with reasons supporting an alternative judgment. Display preference contrasted two highly upvoted comments supporting the same judgment with two representing opposing judgments, plus a no-preference option.

Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results} presents responses separately for readers
whose final judgments agree with the majority and those whose judgments
agree with the minority. This separation makes visible opposing patterns
in certainty and responses to Comment~2 that a pooled mean would obscure.
The majority-aligned groups contain $30$ readers in Two Majority and $31$
in Majority + Minority; the minority-aligned groups contain $10$ and $9$,
respectively. Thus, the proportion whose final judgment agrees with the
majority is descriptively similar across conditions ($75.0\%$ versus
$77.5\%$), providing context for differences in reported considerations
without establishing equivalence in judgment distributions.
Alignment was measured after exposure, so these groups
support descriptive comparisons rather than estimates of effects within
pre-existing judgment groups. We describe the figure from top to bottom.
Throughout, C1 is the majority comment shared across conditions, while
C2 is a second majority comment in Two Majority and a minority comment in
Majority + Minority.

\runinheading{Judgment certainty differs by reported alignment.}
At the top of Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results}, majority-aligned readers
report lower mean certainty in Majority + Minority than in Two Majority
($2.61$ versus $3.43$ on the four-point scale; unadjusted $p<.001$).
Minority-aligned readers show the opposite descriptive pattern: mean
certainty is higher in Majority + Minority ($2.89$ versus $2.30$), although
this difference is not statistically significant ($p=.299$). These are
between-condition differences in final responses, not changes in
individual readers' certainty.

\runinheading{Both judgment groups report a broader range of moral considerations.}
The next panel shows higher mean ratings for the discussion-level range
of moral considerations in Majority + Minority in both groups. Among
majority-aligned readers, the mean is $4.80$ versus $3.93$ in Two Majority
on the seven-point scale ($p=.020$); among minority-aligned readers, it
is $5.22$ versus $4.53$ ($p=.300$). Thus, the direction of this contrast
is shared across judgment groups, unlike certainty. Readers can report
a broader range of considerations while still reaching a judgment that
agrees with the majority.

\runinheading{The shared majority comment receives higher influence ratings in the mixed display.}
The influence panel shows a notable pattern for C1: despite being held
constant within each scenario, it receives higher mean influence ratings
in Majority + Minority in both judgment groups. Majority-aligned readers
rate its influence at $2.94$ versus $2.53$ in Two Majority; minority-aligned
readers rate it at $2.44$ versus $1.50$. These differences are descriptive
($p=.227$ and $p=.082$, respectively). The pattern suggests that readers'
reported use of a comment may depend on the other thread presented
alongside it, even when their final judgment opposes that comment.

C2 shows a different pattern. Its mean influence is lower in Majority +
Minority among majority-aligned readers ($1.74$ versus $2.37$; $p=.030$),
but higher among minority-aligned readers ($2.67$ versus $1.70$; $p=.046$).
Unlike C1, C2 changes in content and judgment direction between conditions.
The mixed display therefore combines higher reported influence of the
shared majority comment in both groups with alignment-dependent responses
to the replacement minority comment.

\runinheading{Agreement with C2 follows readers' final judgment alignment.}
In the agreement panel, mean ratings for C1 are similar across conditions:
$4.29$ in Majority + Minority versus $4.37$ in Two Majority among
majority-aligned readers, and $2.56$ versus $2.70$ among minority-aligned
readers. Neither contrast is significant ($p=.709$ and $p=.823$).
The higher influence ratings for C1 thus occur without correspondingly
higher agreement ratings. For C2, majority-aligned readers report lower
agreement in Majority + Minority ($2.35$ versus $4.40$; $p<.001$), whereas
minority-aligned readers report higher agreement ($4.22$ versus $2.90$;
$p=.017$). Agreement and perceived influence consequently capture
different aspects of readers' responses.

\runinheading{Both comments receive higher ratings for new moral considerations.}
At the bottom of the figure, mean ratings for new moral considerations
are higher in Majority + Minority for both comments and both judgment
groups. Among majority-aligned readers, C1 receives $2.48$ versus $1.97$
in Two Majority, and C2 receives $2.39$ versus $2.00$. Among minority-aligned
readers, the corresponding means are $2.56$ versus $2.00$ for C1 and
$2.78$ versus $2.00$ for C2. None of these four individual contrasts reaches
the $.05$ threshold (all $p\geq.115$). Their shared direction nevertheless
parallels the discussion-level ratings above: the mixed display is
associated with higher reported consideration of moral reasons on both
sides, including for the unchanged majority comment. These ratings describe
perceived new considerations, not independently measured gains in reasoning.
For majority-aligned readers in particular, the replacement minority
comment receives lower agreement and influence ratings but higher mean
ratings for new moral considerations than the second majority comment.
Although the latter contrast is not statistically significant, this
pattern illustrates why agreement, perceived influence, and perceived
new considerations should be distinguished: readers may recognize an
additional consideration without endorsing the judgment expressing it.

\runinheading{Relative comment contribution and preferred display.}
Two additional measures, not plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:survey-results},
assess which comment contributed more to the final judgment and which
display participants prefer. All $40$ participants in each condition
answered the relative-contribution item. In Two Majority, $8$ ($20\%$)
selected C1, $4$ ($10\%$) selected C2, $12$ ($30\%$) selected both equally,
and $16$ ($40\%$) selected neither. In Majority + Minority, the corresponding
counts were $20$ ($50\%$), $6$ ($15\%$), $4$ ($10\%$), and $10$ ($25\%$).
Thus, C1 was selected more often in the mixed condition, while both equally
and neither were selected less often.

Within the mixed condition, the most frequent choice among majority-aligned
readers was C1 ($19/31$, $61.3\%$); among minority-aligned readers, it was
C2 ($4/9$, $44.4\%$). The latter is a plurality rather than a majority.
These descriptive choices complement the separate influence ratings:
although both judgment groups gave C1 higher mean influence ratings in
the mixed condition, their most frequent choice of the more influential
comment matched their final judgment alignment. Relative contribution
and the absolute influence attributed to each comment therefore provide
distinct information.

For display preference, participants most often preferred one comment
from each side: $27/40$ ($67.5\%$) in Two Majority and $23/40$ ($57.5\%$)
in Majority + Minority. The corresponding counts were $8$ ($20.0\%$) and
$11$ ($27.5\%$) for no preference, and $5$ ($12.5\%$) and $6$ ($15.0\%$)
for two comments supporting the same judgment. Preference for seeing both
sides was therefore common in both conditions, even though equal
contribution from both comments was reported by only a subset of readers.

For \textbf{RQ4}, the mixed display is associated with a broader reported
range of moral considerations in both final-judgment groups. It also
coincides with higher reported influence of the shared majority comment,
while certainty and responses to C2 differ in direction by judgment
alignment. These exploratory results concern four selected scenarios and
their displayed threads and scores. All reported $p$-values are from the
figure's $16$ two-sided Welch tests and are unadjusted for multiple
comparisons; the descriptive patterns should therefore be distinguished
from confirmatory evidence.
